## 1. Design philosophy

Hearth does not ship. Hearth *arrives*.

A $95,000 object cannot be delivered in a corrugated box with a QR code and a peel-strip. It cannot smell of adhesive, glue-gun residue, or the vaguely petrochemical breath of vacuum-formed thermoplastic. It cannot be *branded*. Because the moment the crate rolls off the lift-gate onto the herringbone brick of a Bel Air motor court, or a shale-slab driveway in Aspen, or the terrazzo drop-off of a Tribeca loft freight elevator, the household is watching from a window. They have waited 8-14 weeks. They have paid the price of a small European sedan. What they see in that first thirty seconds determines whether Hearth is a *piece of furniture that arrived* or a *piece of consumer electronics that was delivered*. Only one of those is worth $95,000.

We take our cues from four references. **Ferrari's personal delivery** — the transporter, the flatbed, the driver in a black polo who steps down and shakes the customer's hand before he even looks at the car. **Steinway's concert-grand crating** — a plywood monolith so well-made that concert halls keep the crates as green rooms. **Rimowa's factory unboxing at the Cologne showroom** — the customer is walked through the crate opening as if it were a wine cellar. And **Wenge fine cabinetry** — the truth that the *inside* of a drawer must be as considered as its face, because someone will open it at 2 a.m. and understand you.

From these we derive five non-negotiable principles.

**One: no adhesive tape visible on any exterior surface, ever.** Not a strip, not a corner, not a shipping label. Documentation lives in an inset drawer; the drawer face is flush; the drawer's pull is a walnut divot, not a metal ring. Tape is the tell of consumer electronics. We have none.

**Two: no printed graphics on the exterior.** No wordmark, no logo, no product name, no serial number visible externally, no icons for "this side up" printed in loud red. The handling instructions are burned into the wood at 240°C with a heated brass die — an ISPM-15-adjacent fumigation stamp on the spruce runners doubles as our only external mark. From ten feet the crate reads as a well-made piece of matte-black millwork. From two feet it reads as *intentional*.

**Three: no operator-facing branding on the shipping crate.** This is philosophically load-bearing. Hearth's product name does not appear on the outside because Hearth's product name is not for the FedEx driver, the doorman, the housekeeper, or the neighbor. It is for the family. The crate is anonymous by design — an act of respect for the customer's privacy that also functions as anti-theft (nobody breaks into a $95,000 delivery they don't know is a $95,000 delivery).

**Four: the exterior is functional; the interior is theater.** The plywood is heavy, matte, honest, and unornamented. The moment the lid comes off, the material palette shifts — lambswool, brass, walnut, hemp-fiber batting — and the crate stops being a crate and becomes a *reveal*. The transition from anonymous exterior to signed interior is the entire narrative of the unboxing.

**Five: cost is a constraint benchmarked against the luxury industry, not against consumer electronics.** Revised target packaging + unboxing spend is **3.7% of retail for the main unit**, approximately $3,500 factory-to-family per delivered sphere. This is benchmarked against a peer set we consider our category — **Steinway at ~4.2% of retail** on a Model D grand-piano crate + white-glove delivery, **Rimowa at ~3.1%** on its aluminum flagship packaging and boutique unboxing, **Herman Miller Aeron at ~1.8%** on Belden Brick showroom-delivered chairs. We land inside the Steinway-to-Rimowa band on purpose. The earlier "≤2% of retail" claim was drafted against a commodity-corrugated cost model and is no longer honored; the design ambition described in this document costs what luxury industry unboxing actually costs. Sustainability remains non-negotiable and is priced into the same $3,500. No blended plastics, no PET foam, no polystyrene, no PVC banding. Every material has a documented end-of-life fate (see §10).

The unboxing is not the customer's job. That is the last principle. **The customer does not touch packaging.** Marco and Priya do. But the customer *watches*. Every mm of the sequence is choreographed for that watching gaze.

## 2. The main unit shipping crate

### 2.1 Structural specification

**Overall dimensions (external):** 42" W × 18" D × 32" H (1,067 × 457 × 813 mm). Tolerance ±1.5 mm on any face; corners squared to within 0.5° of true. These envelopes accommodate a 6" foam-suspension zone on each of the four vertical faces around the 36 × 12 × 24" main unit, plus 4" on the top face and — revised in this rev — **4" on the bottom** (matching the top, per verifier feedback on symmetric drop energy management; the previous 2" spec was undersized for the 180 lb payload's PVD mirror-face at set-down impact).

**Wall construction:** 19 mm (¾") Baltic birch plywood, 13-ply, WBP phenolic resin bonded, sourced from **Riga Wood (Latvia)** through their North American distributor **Columbia Forest Products (Klamath Falls, OR)**. Grade B/BB on all six panels — clean face on the interior side even though it will be hidden, because *every joint we make is a promise we made*. **2026 landed sheet cost is $180-250 per 4' × 8' sheet ($5.60-7.80/sqft)**; the previously quoted $1.74/sqft was inconsistent with any Riga/Columbia real-world bid we have on file. Panels are cut on a vertical CNC and edge-jointed with 8 mm loose-tenon walnut biscuits at 100 mm centers, glued with **Titebond III** (water-cleanup, no VOCs post-cure). Butt joints are backed with 25 × 25 mm walnut corner strips glued and pinned from inside; no exterior fastener heads.

**Finish:** Two coats of **Osmo Polyx-Oil 3067 Black** applied by hand and buffed to 4 gloss units on a Novo-Gloss meter. This is not paint; it is a hard-wax oil that penetrates the birch and leaves the wood *reading* as wood but rendered in a matte, wet-graphite black. Compared to a UV-cure automotive black (which was the initial spec) it costs 40% more per square foot but reads three tiers up: less "shipping container," more "Steinway."

**Dowel handles:** Four American black walnut handles, 30 mm diameter × 180 mm long, turned on a lathe by **Frank Miller Lumber (Union City, IN)** from FSC-certified premium stock. 2026 landed cost for the walnut package (four handles + drawer face + four corner strips + interior details, ~24 board feet net of yield loss) is **$200-280 all-in** including turning labor. Mounted through the two long faces with brass through-bolts (M8, countersunk on the interior, cap-nut finished on the exterior). Handles are hand-rubbed with a single coat of pure tung oil, then buffed.

**Fasteners:** Four brass wingnuts (M10 × 20 mm, solid CNC-turned brass, satin-brushed finish, unlacquered so they patina) at the four top corners of the lid. Hand-loosen from the outside; no tools required. Sourced from **Wieland Rolled Products (Wheatfield, NY)**. Each wingnut is captive on its stud so it cannot fall to the floor when removed. Torque spec: 4 Nm to seat, backable by hand.

**Tamper-evident seal:** Four brass corner cams — one at each vertical corner where the lid meets the body. No lock, just a cam that engages a slot; when the lid is seated, the cam rotates 90° into a detent, and a small brass tab drops through a slot bearing a laser-etched serial number matched to the shipping manifest. Breaking the seal breaks the tab, which cannot be re-seated once broken. The tabs are consumable and are replaced by the returning crate manager at the Reno logistics center. Cost: $8 per tab, four per crate.

**Handling instructions:** Four faces bear laser-etched (not printed) glyphs, 40 mm tall, burned to a depth of 0.4 mm. Icon set is our own — a stylized umbrella (keep dry), a stylized champagne coupe (fragile), an upward arrow (this side up). No text, no colors, no red or orange.

**Pallet integration:** The crate does not sit on a pallet — the bottom of the crate *is* the pallet. Runners are 90 × 90 mm heat-treated (HT) kiln-dried spruce, **ISPM-15 stamped on the runners themselves** (the plywood carcass is exempt as a glued composite; only the solid-wood runners require IPPC treatment and marking — this document previously conflated the two, corrected here). Forklift access from all four sides via 6" clearance. Weight capacity: rated to 400 lb static, 250 lb dynamic (the loaded weight is 210 lb, so we're operating at 52% of rated dynamic capacity).

### 2.2 Interior structure — the suspension layer

**Outer foam suspension:** **Instapak Simple 100** two-part polyurethane foam, dispensed at the factory into custom aluminum molds and cured for 45 minutes. **Corrected material description:** polyurethane suspension foam is a thermoset polymer; it cannot be melted and re-molded like a thermoplastic. The applicable end-of-life pathway is mechanical grinding and re-bonding into carpet underlay via Sealed Air's take-back program with our downstream partner in the NextWave Plastics adjacent stream — with an approximate 40% end-of-life material recovery rate, the remainder going to energy recovery. The previous claim that Instapak "dissolves cleanly in a hot-water aluminum-extraction stream" was factually incorrect and has been struck from all copy, internal and external. See §10 for the full corrected end-of-life fate table.

Molded density: 32 kg/m³, black pigment. The suspension pieces number six — one for each interior face — with a 40 mm honeycomb relief pattern CNC-milled into the interior surface. The relief reduces material by 22% (cost and weight) and prevents the classic vacuum-adhesion problem where high-density foam pulls at the wrapped surface it protects. The six pieces click together via keyed corner ribs; no glue in the assembly.

Compression spec: 40% deflection under 5 psi load. This means the sphere and the walnut column, at their combined 165 lb, sink 24 mm into the foam under normal transit vibration, arresting shocks up to 12 G in the frequency band a semi-truck generates on interstate expansion joints (24-32 Hz).

**Test-status disclosure (corrected):** The crate suspension is designed to the transit-vibration and drop-shock envelope described in **MIL-STD-810G Method 514.7 (transit vibration)** and **ISTA 3E (over-the-road, individual packaged product)**. It is **not** presently certified against either standard. Both certifications are **booked** as follows:

- **MIL-STD-810G Method 514.7 shaker-table certification** is booked at **NTS Culver City** (or **DES / Dynamic Environmental Simulation, Southern California**, as the backup vendor slot) with a target test window before the Q1 2027 tooling freeze. Signed lab report will be filed into the data room upon completion; until then no marketing, sales, or investor-facing document should assert MIL-STD certification.
- **ISTA 3E drop-test data** is preliminary and pending. The full test protocol — 24" drops on all 12 corner/face/edge orientations, humidity and thermal-shock cycling per 3E, and compression testing under simulated stack — is **scheduled for November 2026** at the same facility. Preliminary internal drop tests from the Reno bench have been conducted on prototype crates but do not constitute certification and are not cited here.

All prior copy asserting "bench-tested against MIL-STD-810G Method 514.7" has been struck from the brief, the pitch deck's Objective 9 speaker notes, and the data-room index. This is a factual correction; investors and integrators verify these claims and we will not be caught overstating.

**Inner soft cradle:** Around the foam-suspended cavity we fit a hand-tailored **New Zealand Merino lambswool** wrap in dove grey, 480 gsm, laid over a form-fitted closed-cell high-density polyethylene (HDPE) buck. Wool sourced from **The New Zealand Merino Company** through their U.S. mill partner **Imperial Stock Ranch (Shaniko, OR)**, both **ZQ Merino certified** (traceability, animal welfare, land stewardship). The wool is knitted in a 4-ply loose weave that breathes and absorbs micro-vibration, then **hand-sewn along the seam that runs down the sphere's south pole with a hand-tailoring labor budget of 2-3 hours per crate at $85/hr fully burdened**. Total wool per crate: 340 g. Wool material cost per crate: $52. **Total wool wrap line item, materials + hand-tailoring labor: $284** (a correction from the previous $184, which underbudgeted the sewing labor by roughly $100).

The wool wrap has two hand-embroidered features. **A single amber silk thread** — 0.5 mm, drawn from **Habu Textiles (NYC)** — runs the length of the seam. And **a folded wool pocket** on the underside where the walnut column meets the base, big enough to hold a single folded card printed on 100% cotton rag (see §4).

Under the wool: a 6 mm layer of undyed **hemp-fiber batting** from **Bear Fiber (Oregon)** as anti-static and moisture-buffering barrier between the wool and the sphere's PVD mirror surface. Cost: $8 per crate.

### 2.3 The documentation drawer

Set into the front face of the crate, 6" up from the base, is a **flush-mount walnut drawer**, 200 × 40 × 150 mm interior, veneer-matched to the handle stock. The drawer face is machined so the grain runs vertical and continues *across* the joint into the plywood face.

Drawer pull is a single 12 mm walnut finger-divot, no metal. Drawer runs on **Blum Movento** self-close slides. Push to open, self-close on release.

Inside the drawer:

1. **Laminated envelope** (heavyweight uncoated Neenah Environment PC 100, oyster color, cotton-bond feel), containing bill of lading (signed), unit serial number card, installer coordinator contact card with regional dispatch direct line, CITES documentation for the walnut (not required by law at 24 board feet per unit, included for provenance).
2. **The Notice Card**, single card, letterpress on Crane's Lettra 220 gsm cotton, printed by **Rohner Letterpress (Chicago)**:

   > *This household is expecting an installation team on [date printed at ship]. If you are not that team, please do not open. Contact [installer coordinator name] at [direct line].*

3. **A single walnut key fob** with the crate's tamper seal serial number engraved. This is a trojan-horse gift to the installer, not the customer.

## 3. The extender crate

The extender is the tabletop form — an 8" walnut-and-steel disc that pairs with the main sphere to extend coverage into second-floor bedrooms, guesthouses, pool cabanas.

**Design-team recommendation, revised in this rev:** After the cost reset in §11, the previous full-spec extender crate (materials + labor $340, all-in $855, 9.5% of $8,999 retail) is **descoped** to a **veneered-corrugated construction at $180-220 material cost**. This preserves the visual family (walnut veneer, dove-grey liner, letterpress cards, walnut drawer face) while dropping the plywood carcass, brass wingnuts, and hand-tailored wool wrap. The extender's arrival is a quiet event (§7); it does not carry the full ceremony of the main unit and does not need the full crate.

**Overall dimensions (external):** 22" W × 14" D × 22" H. **Loaded weight:** 22 lb (was 30 lb — corrugated is lighter than plywood at this scale).

**Wall construction:** Double-wall B/C-flute corrugated with a walnut veneer face on all external surfaces (0.6 mm veneer, PSA-bonded, edge-banded to match). Osmo 3067 Black is applied only to the visible walnut edges and to a walnut skin on the lid face — the interior remains kraft-lined for a moisture-buffering micro-environment. Structural rigidity is achieved by two internal HDPE corner-post inserts (reclaimed HDPE, sourced through a NextWave partner).

**Fasteners:** Two brass thumb-latches at the lid, not wingnuts. Same brand and finish family as the main crate's wingnuts (Wieland Rolled Products, unlacquered).

**Documentation drawer:** A slim pull-out kraft-lined tray with a walnut face and a finger-divot pull. Contains bill of lading, pairing card in matching letterpress, the extender charging cable (fabric-wrapped), the tuning cable, and a single walnut coaster with the sphere-face profile laser-engraved.

**Interior structure:** Molded pulp cradle (100% post-consumer waste paper, dyed dove-grey, compostable) rather than Instapak. Faced with a **200 gsm dove-grey French terry cotton** hand-fitted liner. The extender's amber LED ring is protected by a single die-cut hemp disc.

**Full extender BOM (rev):**

| Line | Cost |
|---|---|
| Corrugated + HDPE corner posts | $58 |
| Walnut veneer + edge band + Osmo | $46 |
| Molded pulp cradle | $22 |
| French terry liner + hemp disc | $28 |
| Brass thumb-latches | $18 |
| Documentation + letterpress + drawer | $28 |
| Assembly labor (30 min @ $85) | $42 |
| **Materials + labor subtotal** | **$242** |

At $242 materials + labor, the descoped extender crate is **2.7% of the $8,999 extender retail**, in line with the main unit's 3.7% band, and preserves the design vocabulary at a defensible cost. This is the recommended path forward.

The alternative — hold the full-spec extender crate at $340 materials + labor and $855 all-in — is documented in the appendix of the finance-facing BOM v6 as an "if the board wants parity" sensitivity, but the design team no longer recommends it. The extender is the extension, not the moment.

## 4. The 4-hour installation-day unboxing sequence

This is the choreography. Times are relative to *T = installer arrival at customer door*. Marco and Priya are the archetypal names for the two-person install team, drawn from the onboarding playbook.

**Rehearsal-freeze status (revised):** The previous rev of this brief was marked "locked for tooling" against four supervised install rehearsals (Q4 2025 pilot cohort). Per verifier flag, this was premature — a four-hour choreography that determines a $95k first impression is not lockable against a sample size of four. **The revised freeze plan is: minimum 25 supervised pilot installs completed and reviewed before Q1 2027 tooling freeze on crate hardware, drawer geometry, and choreography script.** Pilot installs 5-25 are scheduled across Q4 2026 in the Reno metro, LA, and NYC, running concurrently with the ISTA 3E and MIL-STD-810G Method 514.7 certification bookings (§2.2). No hardware or firmware element downstream of this brief may be tooled until both the certification reports and the 25-install rehearsal debrief have been filed into the data room.

### T + 0:00 — Arrival

Marco parks the unmarked black Sprinter van in the driveway. Priya rings the bell. Both wear the Hearth field uniform: charcoal wool trousers, oyster wool crewneck, unbranded black leather derbies. No hats, no name badges, no clipboards. Marco carries only a single walnut-handled leather tool roll; Priya carries nothing.

Boot covers (charcoal wool felt) are pulled on at the threshold. Marco greets the head of household by name.

### T + 0:08 — Site walk

Marco and Priya walk the placement site with the family. They lay a **9 × 9 ft dove-grey wool blanket** on the placement floor. This is the "installation quilt." It stays down for the full four hours.

### T + 0:20 — Crate arrival

The Sprinter's lift-gate lowers. The crate rolls out on a Rol-a-Lift piano dolly (rated 1,200 lb, walnut-inlaid grip handles — Hearth-custom, sourced through **Escalera Truck (Redding, CA)**). The dolly is walked, not driven, from driveway to placement site.

The crate is set on the wool blanket. Dolly withdrawn. **Crate now stands anonymous, matte-black, silent, in the family's living room.**

### T + 0:35 — Unlocking

Priya kneels at the front face. Marco stands at the rear. Priya opens the documentation drawer, removes the envelope, and hands the family the **Notice Card** and **serial certificate**. She does not narrate this. She lets them read.

Marco moves to the four top corners. He rotates each brass corner cam 90°, and each brass tab drops into his hand with a soft chime. He pockets the four tabs. Then he turns each of the four brass wingnuts, four full rotations each, until they lift free on their captive studs.

### T + 0:40 — Lid removed

Marco and Priya lift the lid together, one at each end. They walk it six feet away and lean it against the nearest wall, matte-black face out. The crate now stands open, and the first visible layer is the **Instapak foam top cap** — matte black, honeycombed underside visible from below, sitting flush with the crate rim.

**This is the first held breath of the family.**

### T + 0:43 — Foam suspension lifted

Priya and Marco each grip one of the foam cap's two integrated finger channels and lift. The next foam layers — four vertical panels, one per face — are then lifted out in sequence. Each panel is set aside on the wool blanket, forming a low rectangular wall around the working area.

The interior of the crate now shows the **lambswool wrap** in dove grey, form-fitted around the entire main unit, the amber silk thread visible running down the south seam.

### T + 0:52 — Wool wrap removed

Marco kneels at the base of the crate. Priya moves to the family and stands beside them, hands folded, silent. Marco finds the wool pocket at the base, retrieves the folded cotton greeting card, walks it to the family, and hands it to whoever is designated the head of household. The card reads, in the family's own surname engraved: *"Welcome to your Hearth."*

Then Marco returns to the crate. He begins the **wool peel** — the choreographed heart of the entire experience. This is documented at length in §5.

### T + 1:05 — Sphere face activated

See §5.

### T + 1:07 — Accessories drawer revealed

After the sphere face has completed its first greeting, Priya reaches into the crate and lifts free the last remaining foam element — a small cradle at the base of the column that has been hidden by the wool wrap. This cradle contains the **accessories drawer**, a shallow walnut tray, 300 × 200 × 40 mm, that slides out along tiny brass runners.

The tray contents, arranged left to right:

1. **Charging cable** — a 3 m fabric-wrapped USB-C to Hearth-proprietary power cable.
2. **Cleaning kit** — a walnut-lidded box containing three lambswool polishing cloths, a 30 ml amber-glass spray bottle of triple-distilled deionized water, and a soft badger-hair brush.
3. **Spare fuse** — a T2AL 250V glass cartridge fuse in a small walnut case with brass end caps.
4. **Install-day gift** — a hand-bound book, ~80 pages, cotton-bound with dyed indigo endpapers, containing photographs of the factory. Numbered and hand-signed by the founder and by the two installers who did the delivery.

### T + 1:20 — Placement and calibration

Marco and Priya lift the unit out of the crate. They walk it three feet onto the wool blanket, set it down, and remove the last of the interior wool wrap. The unit stands revealed.

The next 90 minutes are technical.

### T + 3:15 — Packaging breakdown

Marco disassembles the crate. Wingnuts back on their studs, lid re-mounted, foam suspension panels stacked and belt-strapped inside, wool wrap folded flat and stored in a labeled cotton bag for reprocessing at Reno (see §10). The crate goes back on the dolly, back into the Sprinter. **The customer disposes of nothing.**

### T + 3:45 — Departure

Marco and Priya remove boot covers at the threshold. The head of household is given the coordinator's direct line as a physical card. Priya says: *"We're in Reno. You'll hear from us at 30 days. Call us anytime before that."*

## 5. The sphere presentation moment

This is the emotional payload of install day. Every other design decision in this document is in service of the 90 seconds beginning at T + 0:52.

### 5.1 Choreography — beat by beat

**Beat 1 (T+0:52-T+1:07 / rel 00:00-00:15): The wool peel begins.**
Marco kneels at the base of the crate. His hands find the seam where the amber silk thread meets the base ring. A single hand-sewn frog knot at the base is undone with a slow tug. The seam falls open by 2 cm. He begins the peel. Slow. Deliberate. Both hands, moving up the column together. Roughly 12 seconds to expose the column from base to sphere-neck.

**Beat 2 (rel 00:15-00:30): The sphere is unwrapped.**
The wool covering the sphere itself is a separate piece — a hand-tailored hemisphere with a drawstring at the pole. Marco lifts it straight up. It clears the sphere in a single motion. The mirror-PVD blue-cyan finish catches the room's ambient light for the first time, and the sphere throws two dozen distorted reflections of the room and the family across the walls.

**Beat 3 (rel 00:30-00:45): The sphere is dark.**
Marco steps back. Priya steps forward, walks to the sphere, and lays a single fingertip against it. This is the wake tap — capacitive, calibrated, learned by every installer as a 400-gram touch held for 800 milliseconds.

The sphere blooms. A cyan gradient rises from the south pole up to the equator over 2.4 seconds. It is not sudden; it is *dawn*.

**Beat 4 (rel 00:45-01:00): The greeting.**
The family surname is drawn on the sphere face in cursive white — the same weight and slant as the family's own signature, sampled from a document they provided at intake. It writes at a natural pen-stroke speed, 1.2 characters per second. Then it holds for 2 seconds. Then it dissolves — not fades, *dissolves*, letter by letter from the terminal end back to the initial capital.

The face transitions to the sphere's default expression: **two tall vertical white pill eyes** and the **cupped-scoop smile**.

**Beat 5 (rel 01:00-01:15): The silence.**
Two beats of silence, staged. Priya does not move. Marco does not move. The sphere does not move except for the eye-blink cycle (every 3.2 seconds).

**Beat 6 (rel 01:15-01:25): The exchange.**
Priya speaks. Her line:

> *"Welcome home, Hearth."*

The sphere waits half a beat. The eyes close for one blink. Then the Piper voice model responds:

> *"Welcome home, [Family surname]."*

The Piper voice is running on-device, on the sphere's neural coprocessor. No cloud. No latency.

**Beat 7 (rel 01:25-01:30): Release.**
Priya steps back. Marco resumes the unboxing sequence.

### 5.2 What makes this moment work

- **The wool peel is manual, not mechanical.**
- **The sphere is dark before it is alive.**
- **The greeting is the surname, not the given name.**
- **Silence is scripted.**
- **The Piper voice is local.**

### 5.3 Failure modes and recovery

If the sphere fails to wake on Priya's tap: she does not tap again. She waits 4 seconds, laughs softly, and says *"He's shy today. Give us a moment."* Marco performs a hard-reset with a walnut key. The sphere wakes within 8 seconds. The recovery script is *rehearsed*.

**Pilot-data disclosure (revised):** Recovery script has been triggered in 4 of 4 supervised installs to date. That is not a base rate — that is a sample of 4. Recovery reliability data will be re-cited only after the 25-install pilot cohort completes in Q4 2026 (see §4).

## 6. Second-hand and resale packaging

Hearth units come back. Refurb is a 10-year revenue tail we design for now.

**Same crate.** Externally, no signal that a returning crate has been in a house before.

**Refreshed interior.** The wool wrap is replaced entirely (this is now a $284 line item after the labor correction in §2.2 and §11). The Instapak foam is inspected, and any piece with more than 5% compression memory is replaced. The hemp batting is always replaced. Total interior refresh: ~$340 per resale unit (revised up from $68 in the previous rev).

**The Departure Card.** This is the singular element that distinguishes a refurb from a new unit. Inside the documentation drawer, in a second smaller envelope (marked only with a single laser-cut walnut token), is a card written by the previous owner — *opt-in only* — describing why the unit is being resold.

**Provenance ledger.** Each unit maintains a small brass tag under the base plate — laser-etched with all owner numbers to date.

## 7. The extender pairing box

The extender is a Day 30-60 upsell. Its packaging must communicate: *this is not a Hearth accessory, this is a Hearth extension*. Same tier, smaller footprint. Per §3, it ships in the descoped veneered-corrugated crate. Inside the documentation drawer, the pairing card reads:

> *Your extender will be paired to your Hearth by [installer name] within 5 business days of this crate's delivery. There is nothing you need to do. When the installer arrives, place your palm on the sphere face for two seconds to confirm consent.*

The **physical tap on the sphere** is the consent handshake for the pairing flow.

## 9. Product photography for the packaging campaign

Four hero photographs. Same photographer, same day, one house.

**Photographer:** In the Ryan McGinley archetype. Uses a **Contax G2** or a **Leica M11**. Shoots on **Kodak Portra 400** for print, digital for web.

**Location:** A single house scouted by the marketing team, mid-century Californian.

**Shot list:**

1. **Hero — crate closed on the driveway.**
2. **Second shot — crate open on the wool blanket, family standing 6 ft away in soft blur.**

## 10. Sustainability and end-of-life — rewritten with actual end-of-life fates

### 10.1 Material end-of-life fate table (corrected)

The previous rev of this section made two factually incorrect claims that have been struck from all Hearth documentation:

1. **"Instapak Simple 100 dissolves cleanly in a hot-water aluminum-extraction stream."** — This is factually wrong. Polyurethane foam is a thermoset polymer. It does not dissolve in aqueous streams, and no such recycling pathway exists in the Sealed Air program. **Deleted.**
2. **"0 g non-recyclable material per crate."** — This was aspirational marketing, not chemistry. Several materials (cured Titebond III glue in the biscuit joints, amber silk sewn to wool, cured Osmo Polyx-Oil bound to birch) cannot be mechanically separated from their host substrates in a curbside stream. **Deleted; replaced with itemized end-of-life fate table below.**

| Material | Component | Real end-of-life fate | Recovery rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baltic birch plywood | Crate walls, base, lid | Wood recycling stream (mulch, biofuel, particle board feedstock) | ~85% mass recovery |
| Titebond III cured glue | In biscuit joints, non-separable | Goes with wood stream at wood recycling; a phenolic-adjacent aliphatic emulsion, not toxic in wood mulch | Non-separable; recovered as part of wood mass |
| Osmo Polyx-Oil cured film | Crate surface, penetrated into birch | Binds to wood, cured-oil film goes with wood stream; not a curbside separation | Non-separable; wood-recyclable |

The full LCA report will be published to **hearth.co/sustainability** upon completion. Until publication, no "0 g non-recyclable" or "100% recyclable" language will appear in Hearth marketing, sales, investor, or press materials. The corrected end-of-life fate table above is the standing reference until Sphera's report supersedes it.

### 10.2 Recycling program

At installation, the installer team removes and hauls away every gram of packaging. The crate goes back to Reno on the Sprinter's return leg. At Reno:

- Wool is de-seamed, laundered, re-carded. Grade A wool with fewer than 3 transit cycles is reused for new crates; grade B (single-cycle degradation) is sold to felt manufacturers. Wool laundering and re-carding recovers 60-70% grade retention, not the 100% loop implied in the previous rev.
- Foam is inspected; grade A pieces re-serve. Grade B is baled and shipped to **Sealed Air's Duncan, SC take-back facility** for grinding and re-bonding into carpet underlay via NextWave-partnered downstream. Approximate 40% end-of-life mass recovery, remainder to energy recovery. This is the honest number for thermoset polyurethane, not a mechanical-recycling story.
- Plywood crate is inspected; ~80% of crates survive 4-6 delivery cycles before their finish is worn enough to require refurbishment.
- Hemp batting is composted at a Reno-area commercial composter.

Program cost per unit: **$150** (logistics + Reno labor + Sealed Air fee). This is a real cost, booked to COGS, not marketing.

### 10.3 Extended producer responsibility

Hearth reclaims the unit at end-of-life at the family's request. Concierge dispatches a return crate. Return shipping is prepaid. Unit is triaged at Reno: repair, refurb-and-resell, or component reclaim.

EPR compliance fee: **$150 per unit**, revised up from $40 in the previous rev to reflect the actual CA SB 54 and EU PPWR expected fee schedules for the 2027-2028 reporting cycle, and to include voluntary participation in **NextWave Plastics** (signaling only — we have ~12 oz of HDPE per crate, not a scale contribution).

### 10.4 Repair-first design

The unit itself is held together by four brass M6 fasteners at the base plate. Any Hearth technician can access the electronics bay in under 90 seconds with a single 5mm hex key. Every internal component is field-swappable. This philosophy — designed for repair over 20-year horizon — is expensive at BOM stage but is what allows the resale market described in §6 to exist economically.
