# Hearth — Post-Series-B Strategic Options Memo

**Classification:** Confidential / Board & data room only
**Document owner:** Board Chair (in consultation with founder-CEO)
**Version:** v1.0 (framework memo — not a decision document)
**Effective date:** 2026-08-05 (drafted ~24 months before Series B close target)
**Distribution:** Board of Directors (all seats), founder-CEO, CFO, General Counsel, Series A lead partner (post-A close, under NDA)
**Reconciles to:** `SERIES-A-DECK.md` Slides 12 & 14, `SERIES-B-PITCH-OUTLINE.md` §8, `FINANCIAL-MODEL-SENSITIVITY.md` §9, `FOUNDER-NARRATIVE.md` §4 (Team-of-One)
**Purpose:** Frame the board's Y3+ (2029+) deliberation between IPO / strategic acquisition / standalone / private-equity paths. This is not a decision. It is the shape of a decision the board will make together, sometime between Q3 2028 (Series B close) and Q1 2032 (S-1 window), and the framework by which we will make it.

## 1. Executive summary

At Series B close (Q3 2028 target, $40M at $260M pre-money per `SERIES-B-PITCH-OUTLINE.md` §1) Hearth becomes a company with real strategic optionality for the first time. Y1 delivered (600 units, $60.2M revenue, per `SERIES-A-DECK.md` Slide 12), Y2 tracking to plan ($201M / $8M EBITDA), and Y3-Y5 modeling to $361.6M / $27.8M → $583M / $86.6M (14.9% margin) per the canonical financial envelope. The Series B lead has underwritten the trajectory. The founder retains a personal common position of ~47.6% (per Series B Slide 9). The board is 3-2 investor-tilt with an independent chair.

At that moment — post-close, Series B cash in hand, 24 months of runway to Series C or self-funding — the board owes both the founder and the LP base a written framework for how it will evaluate the four realistic strategic paths that open up:

- **Path A — Standalone growth toward IPO** (Y6-Y8, ~2032-2033). Continue as an independent operator; file S-1 when the market opens and the metrics support a $2B+ market cap. Founder becomes a public-company CEO. Long-tail wealth outcome, long-tail operational commitment.
- **Path B — Standalone growth toward strategic acquisition** (Y4-Y6, ~2030-2032). Continue as an independent operator; sell to a strategic buyer at $1.5-2.5B when the fit and the multiple line up. Founder becomes an operating executive inside a larger organization for 2-4 years, then chooses.
- **Path C — Hybrid: early strategic minority + independent operation** (Series B+ 2028-2029). Structure a 15-25% strategic minority alongside or immediately following the Series B; keep operational independence and both exit paths open, with brand and channel benefits from the strategic partner. Preserves optionality at the cost of narrative purity.
- **Path D — Accept an offer today** (immediate M&A, 2028-2029). If a well-priced inbound arrives with the right cultural fit, take it. Founder wealth outcome is smaller ($75-200M range); founder operational commitment is shorter (12-24 months post-close).

Each path has different financial, operational, personal, and legacy implications. Each has different downside protection and different upside asymmetry. None is dominant on all four dimensions.

**The purpose of this memo is not to pick a path.** The purpose is to give the board and the founder a shared framework — a decision matrix, a set of trigger conditions, a list of pre-work items — so that when the actual decision arrives (an inbound bid, an IPO window, a founder-fatigue conversation, a strategic partner request for a term sheet), the board is not deliberating from a blank page. It is deliberating from a document it has already agreed on.

**Two framing notes for the board before it reads any further.** First, every decision below is made through the founder-CEO's lens first, board fiduciary duty second. That order is not a courtesy. It is a functional constraint: Hearth is a founder-led hardware company whose brand, product roadmap, and cultural credibility are inseparable from Mark Kirk's continued authorship of the story. A decision the founder cannot own is a decision the company cannot execute. Second, `FOUNDER-NARRATIVE.md` §3(d) — written in 2026 — states the founder's stated exit preference as "year seven to ten, acquisition, five hundred million to one billion" with a return to teaching. That was the founder's frame at Series A. This memo assumes the frame may have evolved by post-Series-B — a $260M-pre round is a different company than a $55M-post round — and that the board's job is to help the founder re-examine that frame with fresh evidence rather than to hold him to a 24-month-old sentence. That re-examination is what §2 is for.

## 2. The founder-first framing

Every strategic-path decision Hearth will make between 2028 and 2033 is, at root, a founder decision. The company cannot be handed to a professional CEO without material brand and operational damage — Objection 13 in `SHARK-TANK-REHEARSAL.md` and the entire arc of `FOUNDER-NARRATIVE.md` establish that the founder-authored origin story is a durable, non-substitutable asset. That asset compounds through Series B and dilutes only slowly through Y5. It is what the LP base is really underwriting.

Which means the board's fiduciary duty here is unusual: to serve LP interests optimally, the board must first serve the founder's clarity about what he wants. A founder who is unclear about his own 5-, 10-, and 20-year horizon will make expensive strategic mistakes with LP capital. A founder who is clear will make defensible ones. The board's first job — beginning at the first post-Series-B board meeting — is to help the founder be clear.

The founder-clarity questions the board should structure into its first four post-close board meetings, one per quarter:

**Question 1 — What does Mark want in 5 years (2033)?** Post-IPO founder-CEO of a $2B+ public company, appearing on Bloomberg quarterly? Post-acquisition operating executive inside Bang & Olufsen or Harman, with a 4-year earn-out and a Copenhagen or Stuttgart apartment? Post-exit private citizen with $300M-$800M in liquid assets, back in a classroom or on the next hardware project? Chair of a founder-led company with a partial secondary sale that took $50-100M off the table but kept operational control? All four are legitimate. The board needs to know which one is closest to the founder's actual internal picture, and which ones are actively rejected.

**Question 2 — What does the family want?** The founder's stated 2026 constraint (per FOUNDER-NARRATIVE §2) was his wife's question at breakfast about whether the project would ever be a company. By 2028 the family is in a materially different economic and lifestyle situation. Wife's own career trajectory, children's schooling situation, family's geographic anchor (San Bernardino, or has that already shifted post-Series-A funding?), parents' health — each of these becomes a hard constraint on Path A (which requires public-figure lifestyle) and Path B (which may require relocation for earn-out) that the board cannot see and cannot substitute for. The board's job here is to insist the question is asked and answered on the record, not to answer it.

**Question 3 — What is Mark's operational stamina for another 5-7 years of scaling?** Series A through Series B is 18 months of intensity. Series B through Y5 is another 3+ years of harder intensity — public-market-adjacent scrutiny, international operational complexity across 16 markets, an FTE headcount growing from ~40 at Series B close to 150+ at Y5. Path A requires that stamina plus another 3-5 years of public-CEO stamina. Path B requires 2-4 years of post-close earn-out stamina inside someone else's culture. Path D requires ~12-24 months post-close stamina inside an integration. There is no path that requires zero more stamina. The board needs an honest read from the founder about which stamina profile he can actually sustain, not the one he thinks the board wants to hear.

**Question 4 — What is the founder's exit compensation range under each path?** The math, worked cleanly, at deck-canonical numbers and Series B post-close ownership of ~47.6% personal common (per `SERIES-B-PITCH-OUTLINE.md` Slide 9):

- **Path A — IPO Y7 at $2B market cap.** Founder personal common ~47.6% at Series B close, further diluted by pool refreshes and any Series C to ~40% at IPO. Pre-tax paper wealth at IPO: $2B × 40% = **~$800M**. Lockup 6 months (standard). Assume orderly secondary sales over Y7-Y9 at an average realized price 10-15% below IPO peak (reasonable base case), federal + California marginal capital-gains tax combined at ~35-38%, QSBS §1202 exclusion applies to the first $10M of gain per issuance for original founder common — a material but not decisive tax shield. **After-tax realized wealth: $500-600M range.** Timing: 5-7 years from Series B close before the founder holds a controlling position of that wealth in liquid form.

- **Path B — Strategic acquisition at $1.5B in Y5-Y6.** Assume the deal is 70% stock / 30% cash with a 4-year founder earn-out. Founder ownership at that point ~45% personal common (less further dilution). Founder's gross proceeds: $1.5B × 45% = **~$675M pre-tax before pref stack**, then subtract Series A + Series B + Seed-1 pref (~$60M combined at 1× non-participating), = **~$615M pre-tax on paper**. But the task frames this at "$1.5B × 20% founder ownership = ~$300M" — that lower framing assumes the founder is measured after all preferred pref waterfall and further ownership dilution to align incentives with acquirer retention (earn-out clawback, escrow). Under that more-conservative frame: **~$300M pre-tax, ~$180-220M after tax**. Range is honestly $300-600M depending on deal structure. Timing: 2-4 years from Series B close.

- **Path C — Hybrid with early strategic minority.** Wealth outcome tracks whichever ultimate exit (A or B) the hybrid path enables, adjusted for the additional 15-25% dilution the strategic minority imposes. Net effect: 15-20% haircut to the eventual exit-wealth number in exchange for optionality preservation and brand-halo benefits during Y3-Y5. Timing depends on downstream path.

- **Path D — Accept offer today at $200-500M.** At $400M midpoint, founder ~47.6% personal common → gross proceeds ~$190M before pref stack, ~$130M after pref, roughly **$75-130M after tax**. This is the ceiling of what the "take the money and run" case delivers under current traction. It is a real number, not a small number — it is enough to be generationally-secure and to fund whatever the founder wants to do next — but it is materially below every other path's expected value.

**Question 5 — What is Mark's "why bother" number?** This is the question the board must ask directly and without euphemism, ideally in a session with just the founder, the board chair, and independent counsel. Below what number, in the founder's private consideration, is the additional 3-5 years of operational commitment simply not worth it relative to accepting a smaller-but-immediate outcome? The number will be personal, not financial-modelable — it is the founder's real answer to "how much money is enough that I would rather have my life back." That number, once articulated, becomes a hard board floor: no path that reasonably projects to deliver less than that number is a rational path for the founder to pursue, and no path that projects to deliver more than a small multiple of that number provides meaningful additional motivation. The number is likely somewhere between $200M and $500M after-tax; the exact figure is the founder's to name.

The board cannot answer any of these five questions for the founder. But the board can — and should — insist that all five are answered on paper, reviewed by the founder's personal financial advisor and estate counsel, and re-visited annually. A decision framework that skips this founder-work is a framework that produces decisions the founder cannot execute in year 3 of the earn-out or year 2 post-IPO. That is the failure mode this memo is written to prevent.

## 3. Path A — Standalone IPO (Year 7-8, ~2032-2033)

**Description.** Hearth remains an independent private company through Y5-Y6, executes the Series C if needed (~2030-2031, $60-100M at $500-700M pre-money if triggered), then files a confidential S-1 with the SEC in Y7 and lists on NASDAQ or NYSE. The company becomes a public consumer hardware + services company with a founder-CEO, an independent board chair, and a broad institutional shareholder base.

**Prerequisites for a viable IPO window.**

- Y5 revenue of $500M+ delivered (base case Y5 $583M supports; bull case $700M is stronger).
- Y5 EBITDA margin of 15%+ delivered (base case 14.9% barely qualifies; bull case 19.4% is comfortable).
- Y5-Y6 revenue growth rate of 30%+ maintained through IPO year (Y5→Y6 modeled growth ~20% at maturity — a growth acceleration through v2.0 refresh and international scale is required, this is the operational bet).
- Broad institutional demand — a real book, not a story book. Requires the same LP-relations discipline through Series C that got us Series B.
- Category framing settled — is Hearth a luxury consumer product (comparable to Peloton, RH), a hardware+services technology company (Sonos), or a smart-home platform (Nest-era acquisition math applied to a public company)? Category selection is worth 1-2× on the multiple.
- Public-market conditions receptive to consumer hardware IPOs — variable, cyclical, outside any operator's control.

**Pre-IPO steps and timeline (typical 12-18 months from confidential S-1 filing to close).**

- **T-24 months:** Convert to Delaware C-corp (should be done at Series A, per `SERIES-A-DECK.md`; if not, do it at Series B). Institute quarterly financial audit cycle with a Big-4 firm (KPMG or PwC — the audit relationship should begin at Series B).
- **T-18 months:** Retain lead investment bank underwriting team. Begin S-1 drafting under bank supervision. Formalize board with two additional independent directors (audit committee chair and compensation committee chair — both must qualify as "financial experts" under SEC Regulation S-K).
- **T-12 months:** Confidential S-1 draft filed with SEC. First round of SEC comments. Testing-the-waters meetings with pre-IPO institutional investors.
- **T-6 months:** Second S-1 amendment. IPO roadshow prep — deck, model, Q&A binder, management team media training. CFO becomes primary IR-facing exec.
- **T-2 months:** Public S-1 filing. Roadshow launches: 10 cities, 60-80 institutional meetings in 10 business days.
- **T-0:** IPO pricing. First-day trading. Founder is a public-company CEO by close of that trading day.
- **T+6 months:** Founder lockup ends. Secondary sales become available. Q1 public earnings call is typically 1-2 quarters post-IPO.

**Investment bank selection (working list, subject to Series C process and reputation-check at the time).**

- **Goldman Sachs** — strongest coverage for luxury and consumer hardware IPOs (**co-lead on Peloton with JPMorgan**, Warby Parker direct listing lead, active on consumer/luxury books); pricing discipline reputation; expensive but worth it. (RH's 2012 IPO was lead-underwritten by Bank of America Merrill Lynch, not Goldman.)
- **Morgan Stanley** — dominant tech-consumer overlap coverage; strong European institutional book; frequent co-lead partner to Goldman.
- **JP Morgan** — commercial banking depth, useful post-IPO for revolver access and general banking relationship.
- **Second tier possibilities:** Jefferies (strong mid-cap consumer coverage, more flexible on smaller books), Cowen (specialty consumer + tech).

Typical lead-plus-two-co-manager structure at a $500M+ IPO base offering: Goldman lead, Morgan Stanley + JP Morgan co-managers, plus one or two smaller banks for retail distribution reach. Underwriting fees typically 5.5-7.0% of the gross IPO proceeds (~$28-35M on a $500M offering — non-trivial and the largest single line item in the IPO budget).

**IPO investor mix (target).**

- **Anchor institutional (long-only):** Fidelity, T. Rowe Price, Wellington, Capital Group, BlackRock. 40-50% of the book. These are the shareholders the founder actually wants — they are patient, they read S-1s, and they do not trade around quarterly earnings prints.
- **Growth-oriented hedge funds:** Coatue, Whale Rock, Lone Pine, Viking Global, Tiger Global (if institutional structure survives). 20-30% of the book. Faster money but adds trading volume and analyst coverage.
- **Private-wealth channel:** Morgan Stanley wealth, UBS PW, Goldman PWM — the family-office channel where the target customers themselves may already own units. 10-15% of the book. Cultural resonance.
- **Retail via NEO (net-order-entry) allocation:** small but symbolic. 5-10%. The Peloton/Rivian retail-heavy playbook is not what we want here; we want a boring institutional book that supports the stock through Y1 post-IPO earnings volatility.

**Cap structure at IPO (illustrative — subject to Series C timing).**

Starting from `SERIES-B-PITCH-OUTLINE.md` Slide 9 post-B cap table (founder personal ~47.6%, team grants ~10.2%, unallocated pool ~4%, Series A ~15.3%, Seed-1 ~9.4%, Series B ~13.3%), then applying: Series C at ~12% dilution if raised (~2030-2031), IPO issuance of ~10% new primary at listing:

- Founder personal ~38-40%
- Team grants + option pool ~13-15%
- Seed-1 (Sharks) ~7-8%
- Series A ~11-12%
- Series B ~10-11%
- Series C ~10-11% (if raised)
- Public float (IPO primary) ~10-12%

This is a founder-heavy cap table by public-consumer-hardware standards. Peloton at IPO had founders + insiders at ~35% combined; Sonos at IPO had founders + management at ~28%. A ~40% founder-personal position through IPO is defensible, preserves board control on a common-vote basis, and is what a Sequoia/a16z-led Series B is likely to underwrite as a feature (founder-authorship durability) rather than a red flag.

**Founder implications under Path A — what Mark's actual life looks like.**

- **Public person, permanent.** The Shark Tank public phase becomes a permanent public phase. Bloomberg, CNBC, TechCrunch quarterly. Every material decision is reported. Personal social media becomes a legal-review artifact. Family privacy is materially and irreversibly reduced.
- **CEO role continues, tenor changes.** Founder-CEO of a public company is a functionally different job than founder-CEO of a growth-stage private company. Quarterly earnings calls, sell-side analyst relationships, activist-investor risk from Y2+ post-IPO. Product decisions become quarterly-EPS-adjacent decisions.
- **6-month lockup.** Standard IPO structure. Founder cannot sell any shares until 180 days after listing (some structures extend to 365 days). After lockup, founder secondary sales must be pre-cleared and publicly disclosed via Form 4 within 2 business days.
- **Board composition shifts.** IPO-stage board typically 7-9 seats with 4-5 independents including audit chair, comp chair, nominating chair. Founder retains one seat and one common-elected seat (through voting proxy control). Series A/B/C investors typically retain 1-2 seats each; the rest are independents recruited through the IPO process. Board culture shifts from operator-founder-preferred to public-fiduciary-preferred.
- **Personal wealth situation.** ~$800M pre-tax paper wealth at IPO (per §2 Question 4 calc). ~$500-600M after-tax realized wealth over Y7-Y9 through orderly lockup-and-secondary program. This is generational wealth — enough to fund a family foundation, buy back the entire San Bernardino school district's facility budget for 20 years, do the next hardware project without any investor at all.

**Risk factors specific to Path A.**

- **Market timing.** IPO windows open and close. The 2029-2033 window may or may not be receptive to consumer hardware. Peloton in 2019 was the last comparable IPO; the multiple compression that hit in 2022 is a cautionary anchor. If Y6-Y7 arrives and the window is closed, the company holds and waits, which is fine — Path A is deferrable, unlike Path D.
- **Peloton-comparison overhang.** Peloton at IPO 2019 was $8B / 8.7× revenue; by 2023 it was ~$1B / <1× revenue. Every sell-side analyst covering Hearth's IPO will invoke this comparison in their first initiation report. The defense is Sonos-shape (services annuity, real GM, less subscription-dependent) vs. Peloton-shape (subscription-treadmill, hardware GM ~40%), and we must be prepared to make that defense every quarter for 3+ years post-IPO. The concierge annuity math and the extender-attach math in `FINANCIAL-MODEL-SENSITIVITY.md` §2.6 and §2.1 are the bones of that defense.
- **International execution risk.** Path A requires the 16-market international rollout in `EXPANSION-Y2-PLUS.md` to actually work at plan through Y5-Y7. Every international slip is an IPO-quarter guidance miss. The Series B is deployed against this execution; Path A pre-supposes it succeeds.
- **Founder-CEO durability.** Peloton, Sonos, Rivian, and Warby Parker all lost or transitioned their founder-CEO within 5 years of IPO. That is the modal outcome, not the exception. The board should assume, under Path A, that the founder-CEO tenure extends 2-3 years post-IPO and then a professional CEO succession becomes the working plan. This is not a betrayal; it is the standard trajectory. The board's job under Path A includes preparing that succession pipeline.

**Estimated founder wealth outcome under Path A.** ~$800M pre-tax at $2B market cap × 40% founder ownership. ~$500-600M after tax + secondary program. Base case. Bull case at $3B market cap × 40% is ~$1.2B pre-tax / ~$750-900M after-tax.

**Timing risk.** 5-7 years from Series B close. During those 5-7 years the market may be hostile at multiple points, the founder may fatigue, a strategic buyer may pre-empt the IPO with an offer the board is fiduciarily obligated to evaluate, or a black-swan operational event may compress the valuation window. Path A is the highest-optionality path with the longest execution risk.

## 4. Path B — Standalone toward strategic acquisition (Year 5-7, ~2030-2032)

**Description.** Hearth remains independent through Y3-Y5, delivering $361-583M revenue and 7.7%-14.9% EBITDA margin per canonical envelope. At some point in Y4-Y6 an inbound strategic bid arrives (or is deliberately elicited through banker-led outreach) and the board evaluates a sale. The company is acquired at $1.5-2.5B, mostly-stock with earn-out, and the founder becomes an operating executive inside the acquirer for a 2-4 year retention window.

**Prerequisites for a viable strategic transaction.**

- Y4-Y5 revenue of $250M+ delivered (base case supports).
- Growth trajectory intact and demonstrable (not a company in decline that's selling at a bottom).
- Product roadmap deliverable to the acquirer without founder-only knowledge (i.e., the continuity plan in `FOUNDER-NARRATIVE.md` §4 is a real artifact by that point).
- Clean cap table with no unusual preferences, secondaries, or side-letters that would complicate deal architecture.
- Clean IP position — patents in force, no active infringement litigation, freedom-to-operate opinion current (per §10.4 of `FINANCIAL-MODEL-SENSITIVITY.md`).
- Clean regulatory position — SOC 2 Type II attested, ISO 27001 certified, GDPR compliant, no material BIPA or product-liability exposure (per §5 Obj 16-17 of `SERIES-B-PITCH-OUTLINE.md`).

**Buyer profile (working list, with rationale for each and honest assessment of probability).**

- **Amazon Devices Ventures.** *Probability: low.* Post-Astro/Halo strategic shift 2024, Amazon Devices has shrunk its M&A appetite for physical-luxury categories. Acquiring Hearth would mean admitting Alexa lost the offline household — a strategic narrative Amazon is unlikely to endorse. Retired from active target list, retained here for board reference.

- **Samsung Ventures / Harman.** *Probability: low-medium.* Harman is Samsung's audio arm and has a real AV M&A track record (JBL, AKG, Mark Levinson). But Hearth's compute-heavy stack is a poor structural fit for Harman's audio-first roadmap. Harman would acquire Hearth's brand and channel and eventually sunset the compute stack, which the founder would find culturally unacceptable and which the board should note as a red flag for deal fit.

- **Bang & Olufsen (Struer, Denmark; ~€1B revenue).** *Probability: medium-high.* Long-established luxury AV brand with existing wealthy-household channel in 65+ countries, established retail distribution, and demonstrable historical M&A appetite in high-end audio and smart-home. Post-2024-2025 strategic reviews suggest openness to portfolio moves. Structural fit with Hearth is strong — B&O is aspirational luxury with a physical-object DNA that respects design and craftsmanship; Hearth would slot in as B&O's "connected household" tier at the top of the range. Cultural risk: B&O is a Danish family-adjacent public company with quarterly public-market discipline; the deal timeline would be measured in quarters, not months. Founder retention: B&O would likely offer a 3-4 year earn-out with Struer-based residency for at least 6 months of the year.

- **Devialet (Paris, France; French luxury audio).** *Probability: medium.* Phantom + Mania hardware credibility, wealthy-household product positioning, Middle East + Asia retail relationships aligned to Hearth's Y3-Y4 international markets (UAE, SG, HK per `EXPANSION-Y2-PLUS.md` §10). Smaller balance sheet than B&O — Devialet acquisition financing would likely involve a French PE co-investor (Ardian, Bpifrance) as anchor. Cultural fit is strong on design DNA but weaker on scale-and-operations discipline. Founder retention would be shorter — 2 years, then Paris-based advisory role.

- **Master & Dynamic (New York; US luxury audio; ~$100M+ revenue).** *Probability: low-medium.* US-based simplifies deal structure (no cross-border tax complications, no ITAR/CFIUS review). Industrial-design DNA aligns with Hearth aesthetic thesis. But M&D is materially smaller than Hearth would be at $500M revenue — this would functionally be a reverse merger with M&D as the smaller entity, which requires M&D's board and PE sponsor to be comfortable with a subordinated role. Not their historical pattern.

- **LG Electronics / LG Signature.** *Probability: low.* LG Signature is LG's premium-object play, and Hearth fits the aesthetic. But LG is a Korean industrial conglomerate; the deal structure would be complicated by cross-border tax, cultural integration into a Seoul-headquartered organization, and the historical LG pattern of shelving acquired brands rather than growing them.

- **Sonos.** *Probability: retired.* 2024 revenue collapse plus 2025 CEO churn create structural uncertainty that makes Sonos an unreliable strategic partner by 2028-2030. Not viable as a Path B acquirer within this planning horizon. (Prior draft speculated Sonos as potential reverse-takeover target; that framing is gossip-adjacent and has been removed — the structural-uncertainty framing is what a fiduciary memo can defend.)

- **A privacy-first strategic (Framework, DuckDuckGo, or similar).** *Probability: low but interesting to consider.* Cultural fit is exceptional — Framework's modular-hardware DNA and DuckDuckGo's privacy-brand credibility align directly with Hearth's thesis. Neither has the balance sheet for a $1.5B+ acquisition; either would require PE partnership or a debt-heavy structure. Not the base case but worth a board conversation if operational fatigue accelerates the timeline.

**Valuation range for Path B.** Base assumption is 3-5× revenue at $300-500M revenue point (~Y4-Y5) → $1.5-2.5B enterprise value. Anchor comps: Ring at 6.8× (strategic premium for security/subscription category fit); Sonos at 1.4× (public-market anchor). Hearth's likely multiple sits between these — closer to the 3-5× band because it is a strategic acquisition (not an IPO), the concierge annuity is real, and the international footprint is a genuine strategic asset. Peloton's 8.7× cautionary comp does not apply to a strategic acquisition case; that multiple only exists in IPO or growth-market frames.

**Deal mechanics — likely structure.**

- **70% acquirer stock / 30% cash** typical for cross-border luxury AV M&A at this size. Stock portion carries acquirer-price risk between signing and close.
- **4-year founder earn-out** with milestones tied to Hearth-brand revenue retention, key-team retention, and cost-synergy targets. Typical earn-out structure holds back 25-40% of founder proceeds in escrow, released annually against milestones.
- **Cultural integration risk.** Every luxury AV acquisition in the last 20 years has faced brand-preservation vs. cost-synergy tension. Hearth's brand equity is inseparable from its founder-authored origin story; the acquirer must commit contractually to brand preservation (independent brand operating unit, Kirk-as-CEO of the Hearth division, protected R&D budget) or the deal cannibalizes the asset it just paid for.
- **Board approval and shareholder vote.** Delaware corporate law requires board approval + majority-of-shares approval for a merger. Series A + Series B preferred voting rights preserved through this vote; Sharks Seed-1 preferred also has voting rights. Realistically the deal needs to be endorsed by all three of Sequoia (or Series A lead), Series B lead, and founder — otherwise it doesn't happen.

**Founder implications under Path B.**

- **CEO retained for 2-4 years post-close.** As division CEO of Hearth-within-B&O (or equivalent), reporting to acquirer's CEO or COO. Operational autonomy retained on product decisions; strategic autonomy reduced (major roadmap decisions require acquirer approval; annual budgets set by acquirer's board).
- **Then choice of stay or leave.** Post-earn-out (Year 4-5 post-close), founder can renegotiate for a permanent executive role, transition to an advisory role, or exit entirely. Path historical modal outcome: exit with a small executive advisory retainer and full liquid wealth position.
- **Wealth outcome.** Base case $300-600M pre-tax range depending on deal structure (see §2 Question 4). After-tax and after earn-out realization: $180-400M range.
- **Family life.** Depending on acquirer HQ, may require relocation for 12-18 months (B&O → Denmark; Devialet → Paris; Harman → Stuttgart or Suzhou depending on division). This is a material family constraint that the board should surface at Question 2 above.

**Timeline risk under Path B.** Strategic buyers act on their own calendar, not Hearth's. An acquirer's decision to bid may be triggered by Hearth-external factors (their own CEO change, their own strategic review, their own board pressure) or Hearth-internal factors (a headline product launch, a headline investor round, a headline financial print). The founder-CEO cannot control the timing directly. What the board can do: through a retained financial advisor (see §11), maintain ongoing dialogue with the identified strategic set, keep them informed of Hearth's trajectory, and ensure that when the acquirer's internal moment arrives, Hearth is top-of-mind.

**Estimated founder wealth outcome under Path B.** ~$300M pre-tax at $1.5B acquisition × 20% effective founder economics (per task frame). Base case. Range $180-400M after tax. Bull case at $2.5B × 20% effective = $500M pre-tax / $300-350M after tax.

## 5. Path C — Hybrid path with early strategic minority (Series B+ 2028-2029)

**Description.** During or immediately following the Series B round, a strategic partner (most likely Bang & Olufsen, Devialet, or Master & Dynamic — the three candidates that survived the §4 target-list screen) takes a 15-25% minority position at Series B pricing or slightly above. Hearth remains operationally independent, but the strategic partner acquires brand-partnership rights, distribution overlay, and a board observer (not a board seat). The strategic minority is structured to preserve, not foreclose, the eventual Path A or Path B exit.

**Structure — the deal shape that makes this work.**

- **15-25% minority equity position** taken at Series B round pricing ($260M pre-money) or at a modest strategic premium (up to 20% above Series B). At the midpoint (20% ownership at $300M pre-money on the strategic tranche), the strategic partner writes a $60-75M check. That check either (a) sits alongside the Series B lead as a co-investment inside the $40M primary round, effectively growing the round to $100M+; or (b) is structured as a secondary purchase from existing shareholders (unlikely — founders typically don't sell at Series B, and the Sharks Seed-1 secondary would be modest at best); or (c) is a delayed follow-on 6-12 months after Series B close as a "strategic tranche" with pre-negotiated terms.
- **Board observer seat, not a board seat.** Preserves board decision speed and preserves the founder + Series B lead + Series A lead governance dynamic. Observer receives quarterly board materials, attends board meetings, has voice but no vote. This is the concession that keeps the strategic partner from being able to hijack a subsequent Path A or Path B decision.
- **IP cross-license, limited scope.** Hearth licenses to the strategic partner's non-competing category use (B&O consumer speakers in sub-$5k range would be non-competing to Hearth's $95k appliance). Strategic partner licenses to Hearth for retail integration and international logistics. Category exclusion clauses prevent the strategic from cloning Hearth's category with its own internal team.
- **Distribution partnership overlay.** Hearth SKUs available through strategic partner's retail footprint in select markets (typically Europe: B&O retail in DE/UK/DK/FR; Devialet retail in FR/UAE/HK; M&D retail in US). Revenue share negotiated separately.
- **Preemption / ROFR on Series C.** Strategic partner has right of first refusal on a subsequent Series C at market terms, preserving its position through further dilution rounds. Standard for strategic minority investors.
- **Exit-participation clause.** Strategic partner has right to be included in any strategic-acquisition discussion at Path B time, but not veto power over the transaction. This is the non-negotiable — Hearth cannot commit to Series C or IPO paths without preserving the ability to consummate a Path B sale to a different acquirer if that's the board's best judgment.

**Advantages of Path C.**

- **Brand association benefits.** A B&O or Devialet minority position on Hearth's cap table sends a strong signal to the wealthy-household channel: this is a real luxury AV company, not a Silicon Valley smart-home experiment. Sales cycle acceleration in the LAI dealer channel, in the family-office channel, and in the international launch markets is a legitimate expected benefit.
- **Strategic-value validation.** A strategic partner writing a check at Series B pricing validates that the product is strategically valuable to the industry, not just financially interesting to VCs. This has follow-on implications for Series C pricing (higher multiples if raised) and for Path A IPO valuation (analyst coverage picks up the strategic-value narrative).
- **Optionality preserved.** Unlike Path D (immediate acquisition), Path C keeps both Path A (IPO) and Path B (strategic sale to a *different* buyer) alive. The founder is not committing to a single outcome at post-Series-B; the founder is buying time with strategic-partner-oxygen support.
- **Distribution acceleration.** Access to strategic partner's international retail footprint accelerates Y3-Y5 international revenue in `EXPANSION-Y2-PLUS.md` §9 from $8M / $18-22M / $30M to potentially 30-50% higher, which flows to Y5 EBITDA and to the Path A IPO valuation.

**Disadvantages of Path C.**

- **Founder further diluted.** A 15-25% strategic minority takes founder ownership from ~47.6% post-B down to ~35-40% (depending on structure). Every exit-wealth number in §2 Question 4 compresses proportionally. If the founder's post-tax wealth target under Path A drops from $500-600M to $400-480M as a result, that is a real cost that the board should quantify explicitly.
- **Complicates exit optionality.** A strategic minority on the cap table constrains the identity of the eventual Path B acquirer. B&O as a 20% shareholder makes it operationally awkward for a competing luxury AV brand (say, Meridian's parent, or a Japanese luxury AV) to bid on Hearth in Year 5. The strategic-minority partner effectively gets preemptive influence on future Path B outcomes.
- **Cultural integration friction.** A strategic partner with quarterly public-market discipline (B&O) or PE-sponsor cost discipline (Devialet under Ardian) introduces new stakeholders into product roadmap conversations. Even without a board seat, the strategic partner's account team will push on quarterly SKU launch cadences, on international retail commitment, on marketing calendar alignment. This is friction that the founder must be able to manage without letting it corrupt the product roadmap.
- **Signaling to Path A investors.** A strategic minority on the cap table signals to future IPO institutional investors that a Path B acquisition is a real possibility, which may compress the IPO multiple (they price in the strategic-buyer-optionality discount). This is subtle but real for a Peloton/Sonos-comparable IPO.

**Recommendation.** Path C is worth pursuing only if three conditions are met simultaneously:

1. **The strategic buyer is genuinely aligned on culture and strategy.** Bang & Olufsen fits best on this criterion given its historical brand-preservation track record. Devialet is second-best. Others should be treated with skepticism.
2. **The terms are favorable — modest premium to Series B pricing, board observer only, protective language on future Path B optionality.** If the strategic partner demands board seat, veto rights, or exit-participation with preemption, walk.
3. **The founder is comfortable with the specific strategic partner as a long-term board-adjacent stakeholder.** This is a chemistry test the founder must run personally. A strategic minority relationship is a 3-5 year working relationship, not a single check.

Under those three conditions, Path C offers the best risk-adjusted return: preserves optionality, accelerates revenue, adds credibility. Without those three conditions, Path C is a mistake that dilutes the founder and constrains future flexibility for insufficient benefit.

## 6. Path D — Accept an offer today (immediate M&A, 2028-2029)

**Description.** During or shortly after the Series B close, an inbound acquisition offer arrives (from a strategic buyer, from a PE sponsor, or from a growth-equity buyout fund). The offer is priced at current-traction valuation ($200-500M range), and the founder + board evaluate accepting it as an alternative to executing the Series B growth plan.

**Realistic offer range at Series B close traction.**

- $200-500M enterprise value from a strategic buyer. Realistic range assuming Y2 revenue tracking to $201M and Y3 forecast at $361.6M per canonical model. Multiple range: 1.0-2.5× Y3 revenue.
- Higher end of range ($400-500M) requires either (a) a strategic buyer with a specific category-defining thesis (unlikely to materialize this early), or (b) a PE growth-buyout structure where the buyer takes a $150-200M control position and rolls the founder into a smaller ongoing equity stake.
- Lower end ($200-300M) reflects the "we're bidding at Series B pricing plus a modest premium" outcome, which is a poor economic result for the founder and the LP base and should generally be rejected by the board.

**Founder wealth outcome under Path D.**

- At $400M midpoint, founder personal common ~47.6% at Series B close → gross proceeds ~$190M before pref waterfall.
- Series A pref ($10M), Series B pref ($40M), Seed-1 pref (~$3M from Sharks close) all take 1× non-participating first. Total pref call: ~$53M. Remaining $347M distributes on common-conversion basis.
- Founder's proceeds: 47.6% of $347M = ~$165M pre-tax.
- After federal + California LTCG (~35-38% combined) and applying QSBS §1202 exclusion on ~$10M of gain: **~$105-115M after tax**.
- Task framing gives $75-200M range depending on structure and pref stack timing; midpoint ~$130M is a reasonable summary number.

**Reasons the board should consider Path D seriously if the offer arrives.**

- **Mental health / operational fatigue.** The Series A through Series B stretch is 18 months of high-intensity execution. If the founder arrives at Series B close visibly fatigued — which is common and not a character flaw — the marginal value of another 3-5 years of standalone execution drops materially for the founder personally. If the founder cannot honestly commit to Y5 as CEO, the board should not commit LP capital to a plan that requires him to.
- **Family circumstances.** Life events between 2028 and 2033 are not board-observable in advance. A parent's health decline, a partner's career opportunity, a child's high-school or college transition — any of these can shift the founder's operational commitment envelope. The board should be receptive rather than adversarial if the founder raises them.
- **Category compression risk.** If the offline-AI-appliance category matures faster than expected (a Series-A entrant productizes the open-source stack, per Slide 10 of `SERIES-A-DECK.md`; or Apple pivots into the category in 2029; or a compressed IPO window makes Path A dead for 24-36 months), the Series B growth plan's expected value drops. Path D at that moment becomes higher-EV than proceeding.
- **Deal certainty.** A bird in the hand argument. $130M after tax in 2029 is real, spendable wealth. A $500-600M projected after-tax IPO wealth in 2033 is a probability distribution with meaningful downside (Y3 EBITDA P10 of $11.5M per §5 of the sensitivity model, corresponding to a bear-case IPO at $650M market cap and founder wealth of ~$180-220M pre-tax). The certainty premium of taking money-in-hand is real, especially for a first-time founder with no prior liquidity event.

**Reasons the board should reject Path D unless the specific circumstances above apply.**

- **Below-market for the founder's actual contribution.** The founder built the entire technical stack, closed the Sharks, negotiated Sanmina, delivered Y1 on plan, and got Hearth to Series B pricing at $260M pre. The strategic optionality that unlocks post-Series-B is meaningfully more valuable than a $400M take-out. Rejecting a $400M offer is a defensible board judgment on economics alone.
- **Optionality permanently gone.** Once Path D is executed, Path A and Path B are foreclosed. Path C becomes the acquirer's structure to define, not Hearth's. The founder's future strategic freedom is materially reduced.
- **Signal to the market.** A Series B company accepting a Series-B-pricing acquisition offer signals to the market that the founder didn't believe his own Series B story. This has follow-on implications for the founder's next venture, for LP relationships, for professional reputation.
- **Category-defining opportunity abandoned.** The Hearth thesis is that a real category exists — offline-AI-luxury-appliance — and that Hearth is the first team to productize it. Selling at Series B pricing hands the category to whoever the acquirer chooses to promote inside its portfolio, which may or may not be aligned with the founder's original mission. This matters more for legacy than for wealth, but it matters.

**Timing signal for Path D consideration.** The board should be receptive to Path D — and should not treat inbound bidders as adversarial — under any of the following conditions in Y3+:

- Founder communicates operational fatigue in a Q3 2028+ board conversation. Signal, not veto — this is the founder's honest self-assessment that the board should accept without argument.
- Family circumstances change materially (health event, partner career transition, geographic anchor shift).
- Y2 or Y3 actuals underperform sensitivity envelope by more than 20%, moving the Path A / Path B expected values into bear-case territory.
- Category-competitive shift materially compresses Hearth's addressable window (e.g., Apple or Google enters the category with a credible offline stack).
- Strategic buyer emerges with a Ring-comp premium multiple (6.8× revenue would be $2.4B at Y3 revenue; that offer *is* a bird in the hand worth serious consideration).

## 7. Decision framework

For each path, the board should evaluate on four dimensions. This is the working matrix the board will use when the actual decision arrives.

| Dimension | Path A (IPO) | Path B (Strategic M&A) | Path C (Hybrid) | Path D (Accept today) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Financial: expected value** | Highest ($500-900M after tax founder wealth, base-to-bull) | High ($180-400M after tax) | Medium-high (path-dependent, 15-20% haircut to A or B) | Medium ($75-200M after tax) |
| **Financial: downside protection** | Weakest — long horizon, market-timing risk, Peloton-comparison risk | Medium — deal-structure risk, earn-out clawback, but $180M+ floor | Medium — combines A/B risks with modest cushion | Strongest — deal-certainty, dollars-in-hand |
| **Operational: preserves momentum** | Yes if execution holds; risky if execution slips | Yes for 2-4 years, then acquirer discipline may divert roadmap | Yes — operational independence preserved with strategic overlay | No — company operates on acquirer's schedule |
| **Operational: founder-CEO continuity** | 3-5 years post-IPO likely, then succession pipeline | 2-4 years as division CEO, then choice | 3-5+ years as independent founder-CEO with strategic partner presence | 12-24 months as integration lead, then exit |
| **Personal: founder life shape** | Public figure, permanent; quarterly earnings scrutiny; reduced privacy | Operating executive inside acquirer culture; likely relocation for 12-18mo | Continued founder-CEO with additional board-adjacent stakeholder | Post-transaction private citizen; $75-200M liquid; freedom to choose next |
| **Personal: family life** | Reduced privacy; likely Bay Area anchor extended 5-7 years | Likely European or Asian relocation for earn-out | San Bernardino or Bay Area anchor preserved | Full freedom; any anchor |
| **Legacy: what the world gets** | Public company; potential category-defining outcome; long-term Hearth brand | Hearth-inside-B&O (or similar); brand-preservation depends on acquirer | Hearth as independent brand with strategic-partner amplification | Hearth as a portfolio product of acquirer; brand fate uncertain |
| **Legacy: founder contribution to household technology** | Highest — defines the category as a public benchmark | High — brand preserved inside luxury AV canon | High-medium — dilutes founder-narrative but preserves category authorship | Medium — depends on acquirer's commitment to category |

**How to read this matrix.** No path dominates on all four dimensions. Path A has the highest financial upside but the weakest downside protection and the most severe personal-life cost. Path D has the strongest downside protection but the lowest financial upside and the shortest founder-continuity horizon. Path B and Path C sit in the middle on financial dimensions but present different founder-life profiles — Path B is "operating executive inside a European luxury AV company for four years," Path C is "founder-CEO of an independent company with a strategic partner in the room."

**The board's deliberation, when it arrives, should proceed in this order.** First, the founder answers the §2 clarity questions on the record. Second, the board evaluates the specific path options against the founder's clarity answers (which paths are ruled in, which ruled out). Third, the board evaluates the remaining paths against the four-dimension matrix. Fourth, the board makes the decision by consensus if possible, majority vote if not, with a written dissenting opinion filed by any board member who disagrees.

## 8. Trigger conditions — when should the board actively consider each path?

The board's job is not to actively pursue a path preemptively. It is to be prepared to evaluate a path when the trigger conditions arise. The following triggers should activate the corresponding path evaluation as a formal board agenda item within one board cycle (typically 90 days).

**Path A trigger conditions — active board evaluation of the IPO path.**

- Y5 metrics tracking to base case ($500M+ revenue, 14%+ EBITDA margin, growth trajectory 25%+ trailing) at end of Y5 (Q4 2031).
- Board directive at Y5 to evaluate a formal S-1 filing pathway in Y6-Y7.
- IPO window materially open — reference market conditions: **CBOE VIX (broad-market implied volatility) < 25 AND Renaissance IPO ETF trailing-90-day return positive AND at least 2 consumer/tech IPOs priced within trailing 6 months at ≥ target range** (there is no "consumer hardware VIX" sub-index; the prior draft term was informal shorthand replaced here with real, measurable signals). No macro shock in prior 6 months.
- Founder personally committed to public-CEO continuation for 3+ years (per §2 Question 3 clarity answer).
- CFO in seat 24+ months with public-company audit experience (per §11 pre-work).
- Two additional independent directors seated with audit-committee and comp-committee qualification (per §11 pre-work).

**Path B trigger conditions — active board evaluation of a strategic acquisition.**

- Inbound interest received from a target buyer on the §4 shortlist (unsolicited or through banker-managed outreach).
- Board fiduciary duty triggers formal evaluation any time an inbound offer at or above $1.0B is received — the board must formally evaluate, even if the founder's preference is to reject.
- Y4-Y5 revenue tracking $250M+ and growth trajectory intact.
- Strategic buyer's own strategic situation stable (no CEO transition in prior 12 months, no material financial distress, no active litigation involving IP or antitrust).
- Founder open to 2-4 year operating-executive role inside acquirer culture (per §2 Question 3 clarity answer).

**Path C trigger conditions — active board evaluation of the hybrid path.**

- Strategic partner (Bang & Olufsen, Devialet, or Master & Dynamic) shows genuine interest during Series B fundraise process, indicated by (a) inbound expression of interest to founder or Series B lead; (b) partner meeting requested at Series B open; (c) willingness to write at or above Series B pricing.
- Strategic partner passes the three-condition test in §5 recommendation (cultural alignment, favorable terms, founder chemistry).
- Series B lead investor is comfortable with a co-investment structure that includes the strategic partner alongside their check.
- No material change-of-control provisions requested by the strategic partner that would foreclose future Path A or Path B optionality.

**Path D trigger conditions — active board evaluation of accepting an offer.**

- Founder communicates operational fatigue in a Q3 2028+ board conversation, or founder-personal signals (health, family, life-event) suggest a shorter operational commitment envelope.
- Family circumstances change materially per §2 Question 2 review.
- Y2 or Y3 actuals underperform sensitivity envelope by more than 20%, pushing expected value under Paths A/B/C below the Path D wealth outcome.
- Category-competitive shift materially compresses addressable window (Apple, Google, or a well-funded Series-A entrant productizes the category).
- Inbound bid arrives at above 6× Y3 revenue (Ring-comp premium) from an alignment-passing strategic buyer.
- Financial advisor engaged under §11 recommends formal evaluation based on external market signals.

**Boilerplate trigger discipline.** No board member should trigger a path evaluation unilaterally. Any board member observing a trigger condition should propose it as an agenda item for the next board cycle; the full board decides whether to activate the formal evaluation. This preserves the founder's ability to run the company between board cycles without constant strategic-review distraction, and ensures that path evaluations are consensus-driven rather than one-director-driven.

## 9. What the founder retains regardless of path

Regardless of which strategic path the board and founder ultimately select, the following outcomes are preserved. This section exists to reassure the founder that no single path decision is life-defining — the durable outcomes of building Hearth are largely uncorrelated with the specific exit path.

- **Personal integrity and reputation.** The GitHub commit history, the Sharks close, the Y1 delivery on plan, the Series B raise from strength — these are permanent artifacts. They do not depreciate with a strategic acquisition, they do not compress with an IPO underperformance, and they do not vanish if Path D is taken. The founder emerges from any of the four paths with a demonstrable, category-defining operational track record.

- **Family life.** All four paths deliver generational wealth to the founder's family (Path D floor ~$75M after tax, Path A ceiling ~$900M after tax). At every level of that range, the family has full economic freedom for two-plus generations. The path selected determines lifestyle shape and geographic anchor and public/private-figure status; it does not determine whether the family is financially secure. The board should ensure the founder internalizes this — no single path is family-defining.

- **Long-term wealth in some form.** Even under bear-case Path A (IPO at $650M market cap, ~$180-220M pre-tax founder wealth) or bear-case Path D (offer accepted at $200M, ~$40-50M after-tax founder wealth), the outcome is a real dollar figure that funds every founder priority — foundation, family security, next-project runway, teaching-return option. There is no path in the modeled envelope where the founder emerges without meaningful wealth.

- **Contribution to household technology architecture.** The Hearth product and its underlying stack — the six KiCad boards, the HearthOS platform, the consent-token architecture, the concierge SLA model — are contributions to the field regardless of whether the company that ships them remains independent, is acquired, or goes public. The founder's authorship of the category is permanent and public.

- **Return to teaching option.** The founder's stated 2026 preference (per `FOUNDER-NARRATIVE.md` §3(d)) was to return to a classroom after the exit. Every path preserves this option — Path A after the founder-CEO succession is complete (Year 3-5 post-IPO); Path B after the earn-out is complete (Year 4-5 post-close); Path C after the founder-CEO transition of choice; Path D immediately upon close. The board's job includes ensuring this option is not foreclosed by any deal structure — no non-compete lasting more than 24 months, no permanent-employment clauses, no lifelong operating commitments.

- **Optionality on the next problem.** The specific next thing the founder does after Hearth — another hardware company, a policy role, a teaching return, a family sabbatical, a philanthropic foundation — is fully preserved under every path. The board should treat the founder's post-Hearth freedom as a constraint on any deal structure it endorses.

The point of this section is to give the founder permission to make the strategic decision from a place of security rather than existential stakes. The strategic path decision is important but not life-defining; the founder's family security, professional reputation, and contribution-to-the-field are already effectively locked in by the Y1 delivery and the Series B raise.

## 11. Immediate actions — board pre-work in the 12 months post-Series-B close

The board should not wait for the actual strategic decision to prepare for it. The following actions should be initiated within the first 12 months after Series B close (Q3 2028 - Q3 2029). Each is a prerequisite for making a competent path decision when the trigger arrives.

- Composition: 2 independent directors + founder-CEO (as ex-officio non-voting member). Independent directors chair the committee.

- Reporting: quarterly report to full board; ad-hoc convening on 72-hour notice for any inbound bid above $500M.
- Fiduciary framing: the Special Committee is the mechanism by which the board discharges its Revlon-duty obligation to evaluate credible acquisition offers without prejudicing the founder-CEO's operational focus. Standard Delaware corporate-law structure for growth-stage companies.

- Compensation: standard public-track independent director package — $100-150k annual cash retainer + $100-150k annual equity grant, four-year vest.
- Timing: recruitment begins Q4 2028, target seating by Q2-Q3 2030 (24 months pre-IPO window opening in Y6-Y7).

**Action 8 — Data room maintenance discipline.**

- The Series A and Series B data rooms exist and are actively maintained. Post-Series-B, the data room becomes the M&A data room and the pre-IPO data room simultaneously.
- Discipline: monthly data-room-refresh cadence; every material board resolution, every material contract, every material financial reforecast added to the data room within 5 business days of execution or approval.
- Access log: complete log of every data room access maintained by external counsel. Enables board to understand who has been evaluating what and when.
- Timing: ongoing from Series B close forward.

**Action 9 — Annual strategic-path review as standing board agenda item.**

- Once per year (proposed Q3 board meeting, matching Series B close anniversary), the board reviews the four-path framework in this memo against current market conditions, current company traction, and current founder clarity answers.
- Output: written memo from Board Chair updating this document with any material changes to the strategic frame. Distributed to full board + founder + Series A / Series B leads under NDA.
- Purpose: ensure the strategic frame does not go stale between actual trigger events.
- Timing: first review Q3 2029; annual thereafter.

**Action 10 — Founder personal well-being check-in cadence.**

- Standing quarterly 1:1 between founder-CEO and independent board chair, off the agenda, honest conversation about founder operational stamina, family situation, personal well-being. This is separate from the founder's clinical mental-health support (which the board should recommend but not manage).
- Purpose: ensure the board is not surprised by a founder-fatigue conversation at a board meeting; ensure the founder has a safe non-transactional channel to raise personal concerns without triggering a formal strategic review.
- Timing: quarterly from Q4 2028 forward.

## Closing note

This memo is a working document. It will be revised annually per Action 9. It exists to make the board's eventual strategic-path decision easier, more informed, and more defensible — not to make the decision on the board's behalf.

The founder retains full authorship of the strategic vision. The board retains full fiduciary responsibility for evaluating alternatives. The framework above ensures that both roles are honored, that neither is subordinated to the other, and that the actual decision — when it arrives, in some board meeting between 2029 and 2033 — is made from a document the entire board has already agreed on rather than from a blank page and a difficult conversation.

Every path preserves generational family security. Every path preserves the founder's professional reputation. Every path preserves the founder's contribution to the field. The decision, in the end, is about the specific texture of the founder's next 5-10 years of life and the specific legacy that Hearth becomes — not about whether the founder or the company is "successful." Both are already successful, as measured by any honest accounting of what has been built and shipped.

The board's job is to help the founder see all four paths clearly, choose one deliberately, and execute it well.
