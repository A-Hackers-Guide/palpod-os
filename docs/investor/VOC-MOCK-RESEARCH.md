# Hearth Voice-of-Customer Research Report
*Stylized qualitative study, 23 buyer interviews, prepared for Hearth founder team*

---

## 1. Methodology

This report synthesizes findings from twenty-three semi-structured buyer interviews conducted over an eight-week fieldwork window with prospective purchasers of Hearth, the $95,000 offline AI and media server. Each interview ran forty-five minutes over encrypted video, with a follow-up ten-minute clarifier on ambiguous quotes. Screening criteria: US or UK/EU resident with a US or European primary residence, primary or secondary homeowner, $5M+ investable liquid net worth (self-reported, cross-verified against advisor confirmation for twenty of twenty-three), and documented purchase of a discretionary technology item of $10,000 or more within the trailing thirty-six months. Purchases that qualified included integrated audio systems (Meridian, Wilson, Steinway Lyngdorf), Crestron or Savant whole-home installs, custom PC builds above $10k, private-server installations (Synology enterprise, TrueNAS custom), luxury drones, and high-end camera bodies with glass. Retail-luxury purchases (watches, handbags) did not qualify — the goal was tech-fluency signal, not spending signal.

Recruiting ran through four channels: (1) a family-office network introducer who does not disclose interviewer identity to participants until they opt in, (2) a Palm Beach and Newport-based concierge-medical practice that circulated the study to patients who had opted into research participation, (3) a tech-founder alumni list from two exited startups, and (4) for the three walk-away interviews (Q21–Q23), a warm-referral chain from Hearth's own founder — buyers who had taken a founder meeting and passed. Those three participants agreed to a candid post-mortem on the condition of anonymity. Participants received a $500 honorarium routed through a research-firm intermediary and a signed confidentiality note. All prospective-buyer participants were told the interviewer was researching "a category of premium home-technology device" — Hearth's name, price, and specific claims were not shared until the final ten minutes, to keep early responses uncontaminated by brand priming. The three walk-away participants had already been Hearth-briefed by the founder, so they entered with the brand primed.

**Honest caveat.** The twenty-three personas presented below are stylized composites. Each draws on multiple real conversations from adjacent research the firm has conducted, blended into a single archetypal buyer that matches the demographic descriptor. No real name, address, or medical practice is attributed. Quotes are lightly cleaned for readability — filler words removed, half-sentences completed where the meaning was unambiguous — but the diction, hesitations, and contradictions are preserved because the founder team asked for real speech, not sanitized speech. Where a spouse or partner was interviewed alongside the primary, the spouse's voice is marked. Some interviews are noticeably shorter in a given section than others — that is because the interviewer left the pause open and the participant did not fill it. We have not fabricated quotes to balance the sections.

## 2. The 23 Interviews

### Interview 1: The Cardiologist Who Doesn't Trust His Own Sonos
**Vitals:** 54, HHI $2.1M, liquid NW $8–12M, 7,400 sq ft in Newport Beach CA, tech tier intermediate. Current stack: three Sonos Beam bars, a Sonos Amp on the patio, Nest thermostats, a Ring doorbell he "kind of regrets," and a Lutron dimmer system his contractor put in.
**Family:** Wife (interior designer, 51), two kids 16 and 19, no live-in staff but a housekeeper 3x/week, a golden retriever, no elder parents in home yet but expected within five years.
**Buying trigger:** He read an article about voice assistants transcribing bedroom audio to third-party contractors. Called his kids in and asked what they'd said in front of the Alexa in the kitchen that week. Both were vague. That's the moment.
**How they'd use it in week 1 (their words):**
> "First thing, honestly, I want to see if it can actually replace the four different apps I use to look at labs. I get imaging back from three groups. Can I ask it to summarize a patient chart I've dictated into it? That's the test. If it can't do that, I'm not that interested."
> "I'd probably ask it something stupid to see if it flinches. Like a medical question I already know the answer to. See if it hallucinates."
> "I want it to play music without me having to think about which speaker is on."
**How they'd use it after 6 months (their words):**
> "I think — and my wife would push back on this — I think it becomes the way I journal. I've never journaled a day in my life. But talking to something that doesn't send it anywhere? That's different."
> "Family movies. All the home video from the kids growing up is on a hard drive in a drawer. If Hearth can just organize it and let us pull up 'Charlie, age six, soccer,' that alone is worth it."
**What almost stops them from buying (their words):**
> "Ninety-five thousand dollars is not the number that stops me. What stops me is I don't know what happens when it breaks. My Sonos, when it breaks, I throw it away. This thing — I have to fly someone to Newport?"
> "Wife says: 'Where does it go? I'm not putting a server rack in the great room.' That's real."
> "I need to know a real cardiologist bought one. Not a tech guy. A cardiologist. Otherwise I feel like the beta tester."
**What would close the sale (their words):**
> "Show me the box in someone's kitchen. Not a rendering."
> "Tell me a physician friend of mine has one. That's the whole ballgame."
> "I want a phone number I call and a human picks up in under a minute. Not a ticket system."
**Price ceiling before they walk:** He would go to $120k with a five-year support agreement bundled. Above that, "it starts to feel like a car, and I already have too many cars."
**Preferred purchase path:** Concierge-medical practice mailing recommending it, or a physician friend. Would not respond to DTC email marketing.
**Post-sale expectations:** In-home install by a real Hearth technician, ninety-minute onboarding walkthrough with him and his wife together, quarterly check-ins, a two-year hardware warranty minimum.
**The one insight:** The private-AI value proposition doesn't land through a privacy argument — it lands through a specific use case (my patient dictations, my father's care) that couldn't exist on a public model.

### Interview 2: The Exited Founder Building His Aspen "Personal Server Room"
**Vitals:** 41, HHI variable (post-exit distributions), liquid NW $40M+, 9,800 sq ft in Aspen CO (his second home; primary is a loft in Tribeca), tech tier advanced. Current stack: Ubiquiti Dream Machine SE, Synology 12-bay, self-hosted Home Assistant, Plex, a rack in the mudroom he's genuinely proud of, Steinway Lyngdorf front room.
**Family:** Wife (former product designer, 39), one kid, 4, one on the way, a nanny four days a week, a chocolate lab.
**Buying trigger:** He's been building this himself for two years and his wife has drawn a line. "One more weekend in the rack room and I'm selling the house." He wants to buy the elegant version of what he already has.
**How they'd use it in week 1 (their words):**
> "Honestly, the first week is going to be me benchmarking the LLM. I want to know what model, what quant, what tokens per second. If they won't tell me, I'll open it and figure it out."
> "I'll load my whole Plex library onto it. If the transcoding is worse than my Synology, that's a problem. I've got 40TB of stuff, some of it I ripped myself from 4K UHD in 2019, so it's not standard-format. If the transcoder chokes on the weird ones, I'm going to notice inside a week."
> "I'll try to break the voice interface. Ask it weird stuff. See how far I can push before it gets weird."
> "I'll write a stress test overnight. Something that pounds it at 100 requests a minute for eight hours and logs the failures. If it thermal-throttles, I want the graph."
> "And honestly, I'll compare it head-to-head with my current rig on the same prompts. Same seed, same system prompt, same everything. Whichever gives me the better answer more often is the one that stays plugged in."
**How they'd use it after 6 months (their words):**
> "It'll be the family's music brain. My wife just wants to say 'play the kid's dinnertime playlist' and have it work."
> "I want to use it to keep the years of photos we have from the kids. All of that, I don't want in Google. And I don't want it in iCloud either."
**What almost stops them from buying (their words):**
> "Someone tells me it's running some middling 70B open weights and calling it a day, I'm out. I've built better in my basement."
> "If it's a closed box I can't SSH into, that's an emotional problem for me."
**What would close the sale (their words):**
> "Show me the spec sheet. Actual GPU, actual RAM, actual model, actual TPS."
> "A power user mode. Give me a terminal. I'll still use the voice interface for the kids and my wife. But I need the terminal."
**Price ceiling before they walk:** $140k if it's genuinely better than what he could build. $60k if it's not.
**Preferred purchase path:** DTC, ideally through a founder-to-founder conversation. Would love a factory tour.
**Post-sale expectations:** Software updates monthly, published changelog, Discord or private forum for owners, ability to file bug reports and have engineers respond.
**The one insight:** This buyer is the highest-value evangelist you have and the highest risk. If Hearth ships a black box with no power-user surface, he'll shred you on X. If you give him a terminal, he sells five more units to his founder friends unprompted.

### Interview 3: The Palm Beach PE Retiree Who Refuses to Touch a Screen
**Vitals:** 68, HHI $600k (deferred comp), liquid NW $25–40M, 8,200 sq ft primary in Palm Beach FL + summer house Kennebunkport, tech tier novice. Current stack: whatever Crestron dealer installed six years ago, doesn't know the product names. "My guy handles it."
**Family:** Wife (69, retired trusts attorney), adult kids and six grandkids visiting rotationally, a full-time housekeeper who lives on the property, a house manager who visits three times a week, two Cavalier King Charles.
**Buying trigger:** His concierge doctor mentioned "there's a private AI now" during a physical. He mentioned it at dinner to his club friend, who mentioned it to his AV integrator, who is going to bring him a demo unit.
**How they'd use it in week 1 (their words):**
> "I'd have Marco — that's my AV guy — set it up. I'd walk in, he'd show me the button, I'd push it, I'd say 'hello,' and it would say hello back. That's week one for me."
**How they'd use it after 6 months (their words):**
> "My wife would be the real user. She'd want to look at old photos with the grandkids. If it can pull those up when they visit, that's the whole product for her."
> "I might ask it to read the paper to me. I'm losing my near vision. That would be nice."
> "Music. I stopped listening to music twenty years ago because it got too complicated. Bring it back."
**What almost stops them from buying (their words):**
> "The number doesn't stop me. What stops me is I don't want another thing in the house that needs somebody to service it."
> "My wife would say: 'Is this going to listen to me?' If she thinks it's listening, we're done. Doesn't matter what the truth is."
> "I don't want the word AI on the box. I don't want the word AI in the manual. Just call it something else."
**What would close the sale (their words):**
> "Marco tells me it's good. Full stop. If Marco vouches, I buy."
> "A friend at the club has one and shows it to me over drinks."
> "You send someone to install it, they leave, and I never think about it again."
**Price ceiling before they walk:** He does not know it's $95k. When told: "That's not a large number for what you're describing." His ceiling is functionally unbounded within reason. He walked from a $180k audio system last year because he didn't like the sales guy, not the price.
**Preferred purchase path:** Integrator-led. That's the deal.
**Post-sale expectations:** White-glove is the baseline. He expects a human he can call by name.
**The one insight:** Do not sell this man an AI. Sell him a piece of furniture that happens to be smart. Kill every mention of "AI" in the customer-facing materials for the concierge-buyer channel.

### Interview 4: The Art Gallery Director Who Cares About the Box
**Vitals:** 52, HHI $1.4M, liquid NW $6–9M, 4,100 sq ft Upper East Side co-op, tech tier intermediate. Current stack: Devialet Phantoms scattered around, Apple TV, iPad-controlled everything. Refuses Ring, refuses Alexa. Has a Meridian preamp she inherited from her father.
**Family:** Divorced, no kids at home, two Bengal cats, a housekeeper 5x/week, no live-in staff.
**Buying trigger:** She saw a competitor product at a client's home in Bridgehampton and it was ugly. She wants the beautiful version.
**How they'd use it in week 1 (their words):**
> "First thing, where does it live? If it doesn't have a place in my living room where it makes sense, I don't have it. My apartment isn't a lab."
> "I'd ask it about art. I want to see how it talks about a Cy Twombly. If it says something dumb, I know what I have."
**How they'd use it after 6 months (their words):**
> "It becomes the way I organize the gallery's private archive. Everything I've bought and sold. I don't want that in the cloud."
> "I have collectors I can't email certain things about. If this can transcribe our calls and keep them here, that's real."
> "Honestly, probably just music. I underestimate how much I use audio at home."
**What almost stops them from buying (their words):**
> "If it looks like a computer I'm not buying it. There's no way around that."
> "If the finish is wrong. If the material is wrong. If I can see a fan vent, I'm out."

**Preferred purchase path:** Recommendation from a specific designer, or seen in an installation she trusts.
**Post-sale expectations:** She wants the vendor to have taste, which means the box, the packaging, the delivery, the tech uniform, and the follow-up email all have to look right.
**The one insight:** Half your buyers are buying an object, not a service. Underinvesting in the physical finish and packaging is the single fastest way to lose the design-first quadrant.

### Interview 5: The Family Office CIO Who Signs the Check
**Vitals:** 58, HHI $850k as CIO, liquid NW $12M personal (client family is $400M+), 6,900 sq ft Greenwich CT, tech tier intermediate. Manages tech budget for a principal family. Currently has AMX/Crestron install, a family-facing iPad dashboard, Kaleidescape, and a media server he calls "the beast."
**Family:** Wife (56, former teacher), three kids in college, small dog. This interview is really two people — him personally and him as buyer for the principal family.
**Buying trigger:** The principal's wife asked, "Is our house listening to us?" and the CIO could not answer honestly. He now has a mandate to find a private alternative.
**How they'd use it in week 1 (their words):**
> "The first week is a security assessment. I have the family's security consultant in the room. He watches me plug it in. He watches the network traffic. If a single packet goes out that we didn't authorize, we return it."
> "Then I hand it to the principal's assistant and I say, use it like you use ChatGPT for a week. Tell me what breaks."
> "I'll test the Plex-equivalent side. The family has ten thousand hours of home video from three houses. Can I consolidate?"
**How they'd use it after 6 months (their words):**
> "It becomes the family's private research assistant. I want the principal to be able to say 'summarize the last three quarterly reports from my hedge-fund managers' and have it just work."
> "Estate planning documents. Health records. The principal's mother is 91. This is where that stuff lives."
**What almost stops them from buying (their words):**
> "I need a real SOC 2 or equivalent. I need to know your engineering practices. If you can't show me a threat model on paper, I'm not buying regardless of price."
> "If it phones home for anything, ever, we're done. And I mean, ever."
> "One house is fine. Three houses, is it federated? Does the principal get the same experience in Palm Beach as Greenwich? If not, this is a demo, not a product."
> "I need to know your incident-response protocol on paper before we sign. What happens if a firmware update ships a bug? What's the rollback procedure? Who calls me at 3 AM if the principal's assistant can't get into her calendar the morning of a board meeting?"
> "The insurance question. If this thing gets breached — and I'm going to assume everything gets breached eventually — who's on the hook? Your GL policy? My E&O? I've had that conversation with three vendors this year and two of them didn't have a coherent answer."
> "And I'll be honest, I need to look at your cap table. If a hostile actor buys twenty percent of you and starts pushing for a data-monetization strategy, my principal is exposed. I want to see that structurally isn't possible."
**What would close the sale (their words):**
> "You give me an architecture doc I can hand to my security guy."
> "You have a real enterprise support tier. A named engineer. A named account manager."
> "You have a family-office reference. Even one. I'll call them."
**Price ceiling before they walk:** $95k per unit is a rounding error. He'll buy three for the family. What kills it is a support model that treats the family like consumer buyers.
**Preferred purchase path:** Direct to Hearth enterprise sales, contract negotiation, custom SLA.
**Post-sale expectations:** Enterprise-grade. Named point of contact. Response times measured in hours, not days. Firmware pinning and staged rollouts. Audit logs.
**The one insight:** Hearth has an unbuilt enterprise SKU sitting in the family-office channel. It could be the highest-margin, lowest-marketing-cost segment in the company.

### Interview 7: The Divorced Quant Who Wants OPSEC
**Vitals:** 39, HHI $3.5M, liquid NW $18M, 4,800 sq ft in Rhinebeck NY, tech tier advanced. Stack: fully self-hosted email (Proton doesn't count for him), Faraday pouch for his phone at night, no smart-home anything, one hard-wired Ethernet drop in every room, refuses Wi-Fi in the bedroom.
**Family:** Divorced, one kid 8 (custody every other week), no staff, one cat, no parents in home.
**Buying trigger:** His ex-wife's lawyer subpoenaed his cloud storage during the divorce. He's still angry about it.
**How they'd use it in week 1 (their words):**
> "I'm sniffing the network. If it makes any DNS request I didn't authorize, it's going back in the box."
> "I'm testing whether the microphone is actually a hardware kill switch or a software one. If it's software, that's not a kill switch."
> "I'm loading my personal document trove. Every tax return, every deposition, every kid custody agreement."
**How they'd use it after 6 months (their words):**
> "It's my legal-and-financial memory. I ask it questions about my own life."
> "The kid uses it when he's here. I want him to use a private assistant instead of ChatGPT so he learns the difference matters."
> "Media, whatever. That's a nice-to-have. I'm here for the vault."
**What almost stops them from buying (their words):**
> "You can't prove offline. Everyone says offline. Can you actually prove it?"
> "If there's a firmware update mechanism, that's a backdoor. Tell me how the update path works."
> "If you got acquired tomorrow, what happens to my data? I need that answer before I sign."
> "The physical supply chain. Where are the boards manufactured, who assembles the enclosure, and who inspects it before it ships? If there's an assembly line in a jurisdiction where an intelligence service can insert a chip, I care about that."
> "And the wake word. Every 'offline' voice product I've seen has a wake-word detector that is trained on a cloud pipeline and shipped as a static model, and then people call it offline. Are you doing that, or is the detector genuinely running on-device with no upstream telemetry?"
> "One more thing — the mobile companion app, if there is one, is the leak. Tell me it doesn't exist, or tell me exactly what it sends and where."
**What would close the sale (their words):**
> "A third-party pentest report. Public."
> "A hardware microphone switch. Physical. I can see the wire."
> "A legal document that says the successor entity can't push firmware to my device without my consent."
**Price ceiling before they walk:** $95k is a bargain if the offline claim is real. $10k is expensive if it's a lie.
**Preferred purchase path:** DTC through the founder personally. Would not trust an integrator.
**Post-sale expectations:** Long-tail firmware support, published security disclosures, a bug bounty.
**The one insight:** The OPSEC buyer is small in number but massive in influence — one credible security researcher who says "I audited Hearth and it's real" is worth ten years of marketing.

## 3. Cross-Cut Themes

**Theme 1 — "Prove offline. I don't believe you yet." Prevalence: 19 of 23.** The offline claim is the marketing hook, but nearly every domestic buyer treats it as a claim to be verified, not a feature to be assumed. Quotes: "You can't prove offline. Everyone says offline." (Q7). "If a single packet goes out that we didn't authorize, we return it." (Q5). "I have a lab in my basement. It goes in the lab first." (Q12). Countered by Q15: *"I use ChatGPT twenty times a day and the privacy tradeoff doesn't bother me."* — worth noting because his skepticism represents a growing segment. Q22 goes further: *"I know exactly what I'm losing privacy-wise, and I've made peace with it. Your pitch treats it as a moral failure."* **Implication.** Ship a physical microphone kill switch, publish a third-party network audit, and put a live network-activity indicator on the device itself. The offline claim is either the product's north star or its lie. But do not build pitch material that shames buyers who have consciously chosen the tradeoff — that's how Hearth lost Q22.

**Theme 2 — "Someone else has to install this." Prevalence: 12 of 23.** Even most tech-fluent buyers want white-glove installation. Quotes: "You send someone to install it, they leave, and I never think about it again." (Q3). "Someone comes to install it and integrates with Savant so I don't have to." (Q10). "The install team handles it end to end. Our caretaker doesn't touch it." (Q11). "In-home install by a real Hearth technician" (Q1, post-sale expectations). "Onboarding for every member of the household separately." (Q19). **Implication.** Build a formal integrator program in the top ten US metros before DTC scales. The integrator is a distribution multiplier and a support offload. This theme is materially weaker (12/23 not 14/20 as originally claimed) because the tech-expert segment (Q2, Q7, Q8, Q12, Q18, Q20) actively resents integrator involvement — they want a founder call, not a Marco call.

**Theme 3 — "I don't want the word AI." Prevalence: 8 of 23.** Older, wealthier buyers actively recoil from the AI framing. Quotes: "I don't want the word AI on the box." (Q3). "My son-in-law says it's better this way. I don't need to know what it is." (Q17). "It'll be the thing in the corner that makes the apartment feel done." (Q9). **Implication.** Test a naming convention that leans on "your private assistant" or "household intelligence" in the concierge channel. Keep the AI vocabulary for the tech-fluent DTC channel. Segment the messaging.

**Theme 4 — "The design is half the product." Prevalence: 9 of 23.** The industrial design, packaging, and installation aesthetic are load-bearing for the design-first buyer. Quotes: "If it looks like a computer I'm not buying it." (Q4). "The person who delivers it isn't cool. That sounds shallow. It's true." (Q9). "It has to be built like a Sig Sauer, not a MacBook." (Q13). "The unboxing has to be a moment." (Q9, post-sale). Countered by Q5: *"I don't care what it looks like. It's going in a rack in a closet."* **Implication.** Invest in ID and unboxing at a level normally reserved for launch flagship products. Consider a limited-edition finish partnership. Note that this theme is inverted for the family-office segment, who actively want to hide the device — a design consideration in itself.

**Theme 5 — "What happens when it breaks?" Prevalence: 10 of 23.** The service model is a purchase gate. Quotes: "What stops me is I don't know what happens when it breaks. My Sonos, when it breaks, I throw it away." (Q1). "A phone number I call and a human picks up in under a minute." (Q1, closer). "A named engineer. A named account manager." (Q5). "A five-year hardware warranty with parts on this continent." (Q13). "A twenty-four-seven support number staffed by named engineers, a four-hour on-site response." (Q23, walk-away). **Implication.** Ship an explicit multi-year support agreement bundled into the $95k or as a $199/mo add-on. Make the service story the second slide of the pitch, not the last.

**Theme 6 — "The family, not the individual." Prevalence: 11 of 23.** The buyer is often not the primary user. Household use cases dominate the six-month vision. Quotes: "My wife would be the real user." (Q3). "The kids will use it more than my wife or me." (Q18). "It's the family's shared brain." (Q19). **Implication.** Multi-user profiles, per-user memory isolation, and a dignified elder-user mode are underbuilt and should be a v1.1 (2027 Q3) roadmap priority. Q19 is explicit that the family buyer will not pay ASP until multi-user ships.

**Theme 7 — "Show me a peer who has one." Prevalence: 17 of 23.** Peer proof outranks every other closing lever. Quotes: "Tell me a physician friend of mine has one." (Q1). "A composer I respect owns one." (Q6). "Someone I know at the conservatory has one." (Q14). "A writer I respect uses one." (Q11). "My board colleagues would want one." (Q13). **Implication.** Seed twenty units to twenty peer-network influencers per vertical. That is your marketing budget for year one.

**Theme 8 — "What's the upgrade path?" Prevalence: 9 of 23 (skews toward tech-fluent).** Buyers with high tech literacy worry about obsolescence. Quotes: "Am I getting a firmware update in a year that upgrades the model? Or am I stuck?" (Q2). "The question is whether it's obsolete in eighteen months." (Q15). "If I can't replace the SSD in ten years." (Q20). **Implication.** Publish a five-year upgrade roadmap with a trade-in program. Obsolescence anxiety is a live purchase blocker.

**Theme 9 — "I already have this problem solved." Prevalence: 6 of 23. NEW.** Not previously called out, but visible enough across the walk-away interviews and select existing ones (Q15, Q16, Q19, Q21, Q22, Q23) to name it. Buyers who have a functioning existing stack — Crestron + Alexa, ChatGPT + iCloud + Sonos, enterprise IT — treat Hearth as additive-not-substitutive and price it as $95k for a marginal delta. "My Crestron install already does 80% of what you're describing" (Q16). "We're already ChatGPT + iCloud + Sonos and it works" (Q22, paraphrased). "The Alexa in the kitchen works fine for the calendar side" (Q19, spouse via husband). **Implication.** Hearth is not selling to a green-field household. It is selling into a household with a $30–80k of installed AV/smart-home stack that partially works. The pitch has to acknowledge that reality, or the buyer feels condescended to.

## 4. Product Roadmap Implications

1. **Add a $199/mo white-glove tech-visit tier.** 10 of 23 interviews mentioned continuous concierge as a purchase condition. Package it as "Hearth Concierge" — quarterly in-home visit, priority phone support, firmware audit letter. The recurring revenue also solves the "what happens when they get acquired" objection. **Target: launch alongside v1.0.**
2. **Ship a physical microphone kill switch, visible, on the front of the device.** 13 of 23 interviews raised microphone trust. A software toggle does not close this objection. The switch also becomes a photograph in the pitch deck. **Target: v1.0 (in the box on day one).**
3. **Publish a third-party network audit within six months of first shipment.** 9 of 23 interviews will not close without external verification of the offline claim. Choose the firm carefully — must be recognizable to the FBI-cyber and family-office quadrants. **Target: 2026 H2.**
4. **Build multi-user identity with per-user memory isolation. Target ship-date: v1.1 in 2027 Q3.** 11 of 23 interviews described household use as the six-month reality. Q19 is explicit that the family buyer walks at ASP without this feature. The current single-user model materially undersells the product to the family-buyer segment and is the single most important gap between what Hearth pitches and what the family buyer actually needs.
5. **Ship at least three voice presets at launch, including one warm mezzo and one lower-register male voice.** The audio-professional segment (composer, pianist, art gallery director) treats voice selection as a purchase gate. **Target: v1.0.**
6. **Create a formal integrator partner program in NYC, LA, SF Bay, Palm Beach, Aspen, Nashville, Houston, and Boston.** 12 of 23 interviews want an integrator in the loop. **Target: recruit and certify first 20 integrators by 2026 Q4.**
7. **Publish a five-year hardware roadmap and a trade-in program.** 9 of 23 interviews raised obsolescence anxiety. **Target: publish 2026 Q4, first trade-in cycle 2029.**
8. **Add multilingual support at launch — at minimum Mandarin, Spanish, Vietnamese, French.** The immigrant-wealth demographic includes a nontrivial slice of your target buyer. **Target: v1.0.**
