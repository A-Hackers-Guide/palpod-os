## 1. Brand voice + photography direction

Hearth is not a consumer electronics product. Hearth is an object a household lives with — closer to a Steinway in a music room or a Nakashima table than to a HomePod on a shelf. Every photograph in this package must operate from that premise. If any single frame in the final 13 could plausibly appear in an Apple keynote slide, we have failed the brief.

The images must **feel** quiet, expensive, patinaed, occupied. They must feel like a home where someone reads. They must feel like an object that will be inherited. The sphere face — cyan OLED, two tall vertical white pill eyes, cupped-scoop smile — must be photographed as a *presence*, not a *screen*. When the face appears, it should read as a companion in the room, warmly attentive, never demonstrative.

The images must **not** feel like: catalog product photography, Apple hero shots, CES press-release renders, tech reviewer B-roll, or generative-AI illustration. Specifically forbidden: seamless white sweep backgrounds, aggressive rim lighting, chrome that reflects a ring-flash horseshoe, HDR shadow-lift, blown-out highlights on the mirror PVD, and the cool-blue "innovation" color grade that has metastasized across the industry. If a frame looks like a Sony A7 IV JPEG straight-out-of-camera, we reshoot.

Human presence in every frame is handled with restraint. Adults are recognizable only when contracted (the concierge Marco and the founder Mark). Family members are silhouetted, defocused, cropped at the shoulder, or receding from the lens. Children never appear in focus. Hands may enter frame. Faces of household residents never do. This is a brand promise made visually: Hearth photographs the room a family lives in, not the family.

Two North Stars for the photographer to hold across the shoot:

**The one-word test.** For every frame, ask: is this image *inherited* or is this image *purchased*? If it reads as purchased — a thing acquired — we are in the wrong register. Every frame should read as *inherited*: an object that has already earned its place in this room.

**The magazine test.** Every frame should look like it could run full-bleed in *Cabana*, *Apartamento*, *The Gentlewoman*, or a long-form *New Yorker* feature. Never in *Wired*, *The Verge*, or a Kickstarter update.

### 1.2 House style: editorial-domestic warmth over studio-catalog precision

This section exists to resolve a live tension across Hearth's brand documents. The Series A deck, packaging brief, and product-design ethos documents cite **Steinway, Ferrari, and Rimowa** as reference brands. Those references are correct *for the object* — they describe how Hearth is engineered, delivered, and packaged. They are not correct *for the photography*, and this brief rejects them as the photographic reference tier.

**The tension.** Steinway's own concert-grand brand photography is studio-controlled, high-detail, cool-neutral, black-on-black precision — an instrument photographed in isolation on a controlled sweep. Ferrari's brand book is warm but saturated, with mirror-finish paint captured under controlled hot specular highlights and no domestic context. Rimowa under Alexandre Arnault has moved to bright, cool, retro-graphic — high-key, catalog-adjacent, deliberately un-warm. If we apply that photographic register to Hearth, we produce images that show what the object *is* while erasing what the object *does*.

**The resolution.** Hearth is a household object, not a $500k automotive display piece and not a $12k concert grand. We reject the Steinway-concert-grand studio aesthetic (too clinical for a warm domestic product), the Ferrari saturated-showroom aesthetic (too aggressive, too impersonal), and the Rimowa high-key catalog aesthetic (opposite of the tonal register Hearth needs) in favor of the **Aesop / Cabana / Apartamento / editorial-domestic tier**. The reference stack is: Aesop advertising for spare, warm, confidently unbranded image economy; *Cabana* for the room as protagonist; *Apartamento* for lived-in unstyled objects in domestic light; and long-form documentary-editorial family portraiture (in the tradition of the mid-2010s *New York* magazine and *T Magazine* domestic features) for how humans occupy frames without becoming testimonials.

**The rule that follows.** The design ethos — Steinway craftsmanship, Ferrari personal-delivery ceremony, Rimowa unboxing precision — informs how the **object** is engineered, delivered, and packaged. The photography captures how the object **lives** in the home, not how it looks in isolation. Object language ≠ photographic language. The two must be internally consistent (a photograph of a Steinway-caliber object cannot look cheap) but they are not the same language.

**Cross-document action.** The Series A deck's Slide 4 (product architecture) will be updated to note: *"Photography direction: editorial-domestic per PHOTOGRAPHY-BRIEF v2.0. Reference tier: Aesop / Cabana / Apartamento, not Steinway studio catalog."* The packaging brief will carry the same footnote. When any downstream stakeholder cross-reads brief and deck, the reconciliation is on the page — not implied.

## 2. The 13-shot hero brand package

The package is 12 hero editorial frames plus 1 utility cutout (Shot 13) for the extender pricing/spec sheet. All 13 are contracted through the primary shoot; the cutout is scoped to a separate day with the still-life specialist.

### Shot 1 — Hero: the sphere on the plinth
**Description.** The sphere alone on the 30×40" walnut plinth, in a domestic room whose architecture does most of the compositional work. A single window, unshuttered, throws a broad late-afternoon rectangle across the floor. The sphere sits inside that rectangle. The room is otherwise nearly empty — a chair, a book, a wool rug. This is the frame that must earn the price tag.
**Setting.** Palo Alto craftsman living room, west-facing bay window, oak floors, plaster walls unpainted for one week to allow chalk-white to warm.
**Time / light.** 4:20–4:50 PM PT. Warm directional daylight, one bounce card only.
**Subjects.** Product only. No human presence in frame.
**Camera + lens.** Hasselblad H6D-100c, HC 80mm f/2.8. Medium format for the tonal shoulder on walnut and skin-adjacent creamy plaster, and for the way it renders mirror PVD without breaking into hot specular. Full stop: no digital-cinema rig, no tilt-shift. This is architecture photography of a small object.
**Exposure.** ISO 200, f/5.6, 1/60s, tripod, mirror lockup.
**Post.** Kodak Portra 800 emulation base, highlight roll-off pulled to soft-shoulder. Warm bias +150K. Grain: very light, shadow-weighted only. No sharpening beyond capture default.
**Crop.** 4:5 vertical, room for headline top-quarter.
**Use.** Series A deck cover slide, CES booth panel A (render for CES, photo post-CES), marketing homepage hero, packaging outer sleeve.
**Assigned.** Coral von Zumwalt (primary).

### Shot 2 — The face
**Description.** Extreme close-up of the OLED face, cyan-blue field filling the frame, the two tall vertical white pill eyes and the cupped-scoop smile occupying the lower two-thirds. Nothing else. No sphere edge, no room. The face reads as a presence, not a screen.
**Setting.** Neutral studio, matte black surround, sphere on articulating rig.
**Time / light.** N/A studio, but modeled on late-golden. One large soft key camera-right at 45°, minus-green gel to keep OLED cyan pure. Black flag left for shape.
**Subjects.** Product only. Expression state: *warm-greeting* (state 03 of 9). Confirm state ID with product team before capture.
**Camera + lens.** Phase One IQ4 150MP with Schneider 120mm Blue Ring macro. The Phase One sensor renders OLED subpixels without moiré at working distance and holds cyan into shadow without muddying — a step up from the Leica S3 originally scoped, matching the specialist's owned kit.
**Exposure.** ISO 100, f/8, 1/125s. Tethered.
**Post.** Do not "clean" the pixel structure — micro-texture in the OLED grid is a feature, not a defect, and reads as craft at 30" print size. Highlight roll-off gentle. No saturation lift on cyan; if anything, minus 4.
**Crop.** 4:5 vertical, close.
**Use.** CES booth panel B (render for CES, photo post-CES), press kit opener, packaging inner sleeve, deck slide 3.
**Assigned.** Levon Biss (macro specialist).

### Shot 3 — Levitation in motion

**Post.** Portra 800 emulation, warm bias. Add trace motion blur to sphere edge only, not to background. Kill Sony highlight bite.
**Crop.** 3:2 horizontal.
**Use.** Series A deck slide 4 (product mechanism section), CES motion loop still (render for CES, photo replaces post-CES), PR press kit.
**Assigned.** Coral von Zumwalt.

### Shot 4 — The column

## 4. CES timing resolution + shoot timeline

### 4.1 The problem stated plainly

CES 2027 runs January 6–9. The v1.0 brief scheduled the primary shoot Jan 15–30 with Feb 22 delivery — six weeks after CES ends. The workaround (a one-day Dec 18 Anderson pre-shoot for rough booth panels) was rejected by the verifier for correct reasons: (a) wrong tier of photographer for booth prints, (b) a rushed one-day pre-shoot cannot produce gallery-quality 30×40" to 60×48" prints, and (c) the pre-shoot cost was bad ROI against a package that would be replaced six weeks later. This section commits to a clean resolution.

### 4.2 Two viable paths

Both paths use **placeholder renders for the CES booth panels**. Downing Design and Impact Group both maintain in-house 3D rendering teams that can produce photo-adjacent CGI (CAD + Blender + post-render color-and-grain treatment matched to the Portra-800 direction in Section 8) at booth-print scale. This is standard practice for pre-launch industrial products at CES; the panels read as photographic under booth lighting and stand up at 60×48" if produced with proper subsurface-scattering and material-response settings on the walnut, brass, and mirror-PVD assets we already have in the CAD library.

**Path A — Recommended: post-CES shoot, renders for CES.**
- CES booth panels A/B/C/D/E/F/G/H produced as photo-adjacent renders, in-house at Downing Design or Impact Group
- Render production: December 2026, 3-week window
- Render budget: $30–50k (covers 8 booth-scale renders + 1 revision round)
- Primary photography shoot: January 20 – February 15, 2027
- Delivery: February 22, 2027 — full package, replaces CES renders across all channels immediately post-show
- **Why this is recommended:** photographers are not rushed, tonal register is preserved end-to-end, the polished package feeds Series A + packaging + web at full quality, and dealers who see both the CES renders and the post-CES photography will read the swap as an upgrade rather than as a downgrade from a rushed pre-shoot to a polished replacement. Booth ROI is protected; brand ROI is protected; nothing is compromised.

**Path B — Alternative: early-December shoot, photography for CES.**
- CES booth panels produced from December photography with 5-week post window closing December 30
- Primary photography shoot: December 1–15, 2026 (Coral von Zumwalt and Bobby Doherty have both confirmed availability on this window)
- Delivery: January 5, 2027 — one day before CES opens
- **Why this is not recommended:** the delivery timeline is tight; a single weather/production delay in December compresses post-production to unacceptable levels; and the earliest editorial passes will be the ones that ship, when the six-week Path A window produces materially better final grade. Path B is documented here as the fallback if render production hits an unrecoverable blocker in October or November 2026.

### 4.3 The chosen path

**Path A is chosen** for the reasons above. Renders cover CES; photography ships post-CES; Series A pitch, packaging, and marketing site all take the polished package on Feb 22. Head of Brand and Head of Ops signed to this path; renders scoped with Downing Design as of the v2.0 signature date.

## 8. Post-production direction

**Master emulation.** Kodak Portra 800 base across the package, with Portra 400 as substitute where finer grain is required (shots 4, 9). Motion-picture Kodak 2383 print emulation is *not* used — this is stills, not cinema.

**Highlight roll-off.** Soft, warm-shouldered. Reference: a scanned Portra frame from a Pentax 67, not a Sony JPEG. No highlight punch. Mirror PVD is allowed one small clipped specular per frame maximum; brass is allowed none.

**White balance.** 3200K for interior scenes with practical tungsten; 5500K for evening / window-light frames. Never neutral daylight 5000K — reads clinical.

**Skin tones.** Warm, held. Reference: Portra 800 shot on M-mount lens at f/2. Never cyan-shifted.

**Contrast.** Medium. No high-contrast, no HDR, no shadow lift beyond film-natural. Blacks land at IRE 4, not 0.

**Grain.** Present and honored. Light grain in shadows only for medium-format frames; slightly more prominent for shot 12 (pushed Portra 800 at ISO 3200). No grain plug-in that reads digital — use the LUT-plus-grain-plate workflow.

**Retouching.** Extremely light-touch:
- Sphere fingerprints cleaned.
- Walnut grain honored, not smoothed.
- Skin: pore-preserving retouching only; no smoothing.
- Family clothing rumples and household imperfections (a book at a slight angle, a rug lifted at a corner) preserved.
- Sensor dust cleaned. Full stop.

**Shot 13 cutout exception.** Shot 13 is the only frame in the package that permits a cleaner post treatment — it lives on a spec sheet next to bulleted specifications, and pure editorial-domestic grain hurts legibility. Cutout permits: slightly cooler WB (still Portra-shouldered, not clinical), cleaner shadow floor, no visible grain in the on-white master. Grain is retained on the tonal-range derivative used inside the marketing site so that Shot 13 does not appear stylistically orphaned when it sits next to Shot 6 in the extender section.

**Color grading pass.** Single grade applied across the 12-shot editorial package for cross-frame continuity, then per-shot refinement. Shot 13 receives its own utility grade documented as a variant, not a break. Grade must be signed off on the intended output medium — print stock for the packaging shots, sRGB soft-proof for web, DCI-P3 for CES panels (renders and post-CES photos both), CMYK-proofed for the pricing sheet.

**Render-to-photo continuity.** The CES renders produced by Downing Design/Impact Group must be color-graded to match the Portra-800 direction above. Render team receives the master LUT and a set of reference photos from a Nov 2026 test-shoot day (single day, in-house documentation photographer, budgeted separately at $3k) to calibrate render output to photographic grade. When the post-CES photos replace the renders on the website and packaging, the tonal transition should be seamless.
