## 1. Brand philosophy

### What Hearth IS

Hearth is a household appliance that thinks and feels.

Read that sentence twice. The category words are "household appliance." Not gadget. Not device. Not platform. An appliance is a category of object that has existed in the American home for a century — the refrigerator, the range, the washer, the grand piano. Appliances are chosen once, installed carefully, and expected to last. They are quiet. They are large enough to notice, but designed to disappear into the routine of daily life. They are not asked to justify themselves each morning.

Hearth belongs on that shelf. It happens to think — it recognises the voices and faces of the household, remembers what matters, and speaks when spoken to. It happens to feel — a cyan-blue OLED face, nine expression states, a warm amber halo along its seams. These are not features. They are the *presence* the object contributes to a room.

Hearth is a companion object in the household. Not a companion in the AI-relationship sense — Hearth is not a friend, not a therapist, not a partner. It is a companion the way a well-worn reading chair is a companion, or a Steinway upright, or a walnut writing desk that has been in the family for two generations. It is present, it is aware of the household, and it earns its place through decades of quiet service.

Everything about Hearth — its steel and walnut body, its mirror PVD finish, its amber-lit seams, its face — is tuned to the register of the household's most considered objects. It costs $95,000 because a considered object built to last decades costs $95,000. That is not a claim. It is an observation about the category we chose.

### What Hearth is NOT

Hearth is not a smart speaker. Smart speakers sit on kitchen counters, cost $99, and are replaced when the next model is announced.

Hearth is not a voice assistant. Voice assistants belong to advertising ecosystems and are named after women in customer service roles.

Hearth is not an AI assistant. "AI assistant" is a phrase invented by consumer electronics marketing in 2022. It sits on the same shelf as "3D TV" and "smart fridge."

Hearth is not a robot. Robots have arms, wheels, or personality routines borrowed from science fiction.

Hearth is not a hub. Hubs are plastic cubes that connect other plastic cubes.

Hearth is not a controller. Controllers are things you hold.

Hearth is not a system. A system is a diagram in a spec sheet.

Every one of those words drags Hearth into a category built for $99-to-$1,499 consumer electronics. We are not in that aisle. We are not competing with those objects. We do not defend against them, benchmark against them, or reference them in copy. When a customer, a journalist, or an employee reaches for one of those words, we gently correct: Hearth is a household appliance.

### Why category positioning matters

The buyer we serve has $5M or more in liquid net worth. This buyer has already exited the consumer electronics category as a source of pride. They do not brag about their refrigerator; they chose a Sub-Zero because it's what a household of their kind installs and they now do not think about it. They do not evaluate the espresso machine on the countertop by its watts and portafilter diameter; they chose the La Marzocco because a friend has one and they now do not think about it.

Hearth must be selected the same way. Not by feature comparison. Not by benchmark. By category recognition. When a household of this kind is shown Hearth, the question they should ask is not "how does it compare to Alexa?" but "where does it go in the living room?"

Category positioning is not aesthetic preference. It is the entire economic argument. A smart speaker at $95,000 is absurd. A considered household appliance at $95,000 is defensible on the same math as a Steinway B, a Sub-Zero Wolf column pair, a Nakashima writing desk, or a Bulthaup kitchen. The category we occupy is the category that supports the price.

### The naming convention

There are two names, and only two names, for the thing we make.

- **Hearth** — the proper noun. Always capitalised. No article. "Hearth remembers the household's rhythms." "Hearth arrives Tuesday." "Hearth is home." Never *"the Hearth."* Never *"a Hearth."* Never *"my Hearth"* in headline copy (in a customer's own quote, it is permitted).
- **The sphere** — the physical object itself, when the sentence needs to refer specifically to the shape sitting in the room. "The sphere is 240mm in diameter." "The sphere's face is a 4-inch cyan OLED." Lowercase, definite article. Use "the sphere" sparingly and only when the physical body is the referent.

Explicitly forbidden constructions, at all times, in all channels:

- "Hearth device"
- "Hearth unit"
- "Hearth product"
- "Hearth system"
- "Hearth hub"
- "Hearth speaker"
- "Hearth AI"
- "the Hearth device"
- "your Hearth device"

If a sentence cannot be written without one of these, the sentence is wrong and must be re-written. There is no exception for internal decks, dealer sheets, or engineering copy that touches marketing. The naming convention is load-bearing for the category argument.

## 2. Vocabulary

The vocabulary discipline below is not a preference list. It is the language of the category. Every word on the DO list carries the register we chose. Every word on the DO NOT list drags us back into consumer electronics.

### DO use

| Term | Use it to mean |
| --- | --- |
| **Hearth** | The proper noun for the appliance. Always capitalised, no article. |
| **The sphere** | The physical object — the 240mm body, the face, the amber halo, the steel and walnut form. |
| **Companion** | Companion *object*, as in "a household companion object." Never in the AI-relationship sense. Hearth is a companion the way a Steinway is a companion — an object that earns its place in the household through presence and service. |
| **Household** | The people who live in the home. Always "household," never "family," in marketing. A single person is a household. A couple is a household. Three generations under one roof is a household. "Family" excludes; "household" includes. |
| **Concierge** | The human service tier. When a customer needs help, they speak with Concierge. Not "support." Not "customer service." Not "the help desk." Capitalised when referring to the tier as a proper noun ("Concierge is available around the clock"); lowercase when generic. |
| **Extender** | The secondary-room product — the smaller sphere for the bedroom, the library, the studio. Not a "satellite," not a "remote," not a "node." An extender. |
| **Presence** | What Hearth adds to a room. Not "features." Not "capabilities." A room with Hearth has a certain presence in it. This is the correct word. |
| **Memory** | What Hearth builds over time. "Hearth remembers your household." "Memory grows with the household." Not "data," not "profile," not "history." |
| **Voice** | Hearth speaks. Hearth has a voice. When a household member speaks with Hearth, they are having a conversation. Not a "chat." Not a "session." |
| **Reply** | What Hearth says back. Not "response." Not "answer output." |
| **Question** | What the household asks. Not "query." Not "prompt." Not "input." |
| **Conversation** | The exchange between the household and Hearth. Not "chat," "session," "interaction," or "dialogue." |
| **The household guide book** | The linen-bound printed book that ships with Hearth. Not a "manual." Not a "quick-start guide." |
| **Install** | The act of placing Hearth in the household. Concierge installs Hearth. Not "setup." Not "onboarding." |

### Data-room and regulator-facing carve-out

The NEVER list is disciplined in customer-facing, marketing, PR, and public-facing copy. It is NOT enforced in regulator-facing legal documents, audit responses, or data-room compliance filings where specific legal terminology is required. `THREAT-MODEL`, `PRIVACY-COMPLIANCE-MANUAL`, and other compliance filings sitting in the data room's security/compliance folders are exempt from the NEVER list.

Specifically, the legally required terms below are permitted, and in most cases required, in the documents named:

- **"Consumer"** — required legal term in FTC-facing filings, CCPA / CPRA disclosures, and every state-comprehensive-privacy-law compliance response (CPA, CTDPA, VCDPA, MODPA, NJDPA, etc.).
- **"Data subject"** — required legal term in GDPR documentation, UK GDPR, Swiss FADP, and every DPA that maps to the EU controller-processor model.
- **"Biometric information"** and **"biometric identifier"** — required legal terms in BIPA (740 ILCS 14/) written notice, retention schedules, and consent text, and in Texas CUBI and Washington MHMDA parallel filings.
- **"User access"**, **"user"**, **"user account"** — required legal terms in SOC 2 audit responses, ISO 27001 Statement of Applicability language, and the Trail of Bits / third-party audit response format.
- **"Device"**, **"unit"**, **"product"** — permitted where the specific regulatory schema requires those terms (e.g., CE marking dossiers, UKCA declarations, TDRA/ESMA filings) even though they are forbidden in customer-facing copy.
- **"Firmware update"** — permitted in the compliance and audit context (the customer-facing rewrite "Hearth learns a new capability" is inappropriate in a security disclosure that must name the update as a firmware event).

This carve-out is scoped narrowly. The moment a regulator-facing filing is repurposed into public marketing copy — a Trail of Bits redacted summary quoted in a blog post, a GDPR DPO letter excerpted for a homepage press panel, a SOC 2 attestation badge with quoted language — the marketing register applies and the vocabulary in §2 governs. The Head of Brand and General Counsel jointly approve any migration of legally-required terminology into a public-facing surface, and the migration is a rewrite, not a copy-paste.

## 3. Tone of voice

Hearth's voice has six core attributes. Every sentence in every channel is measured against these six. A sentence that fails on any of them is re-written.

### 1. Quiet — Steinway, not Sonos

**What it feels like.** The presence in the room before you notice it. Level, low, unforced. It doesn't call attention to itself. It doesn't sell. When it says something, the sentence is shorter than you expected and lands with more weight than you expected.

**What it does not feel like.** Announcement. Excitement. Marketing-copy energy. Never a headline that has to work to be a headline.

**Example.** *Your household, contained.*

**Contrast.** ~~Introducing the future of home intelligence — Hearth is here!~~

### 2. Confident — understated authority, not swagger

**What it feels like.** The founder of a Swiss watchmaker describing the movement. Facts, plainly stated, spoken by someone who does not need to convince you. The confidence of a thing well made, described by the person who made it.

**What it does not feel like.** Chest-beating. Superlatives. Comparative claims against named competitors. Anything that sounds like it's trying to win the sentence.

**Example.** *It's built the way we build it.*

**Contrast.** ~~The world's most advanced AI-powered home platform.~~

### 3. Warm — Aesop, not Apple

**What it feels like.** A hand-signed note. Aesop's product copy: written by someone who has read novels, in complete sentences, addressing the reader as an adult. Warm because it is specific to the household, not because it uses warm-sounding words.

**What it does not feel like.** Sentimental. Saccharine. Generic domestic imagery. Anything that feels written to please rather than written to a person.

**Example.** *Hello, the Kim household.*

**Contrast.** ~~Welcome to your smart home journey!~~

### 4. Precise — Nakashima, not IKEA

**What it feels like.** The exact number. The exact material. The exact measurement, given without embellishment because the number itself is sufficient. A George Nakashima catalogue: "American black walnut, single slab, book-matched butterfly joint."

**What it does not feel like.** Rounded, approximate, or hyped up. Never "up to X" or "as much as Y." Never a benchmark. Never a marketing-adjective before a number.

**Example.** *38.4 terabytes, all yours.*

**Contrast.** ~~Massive storage capacity for all your media needs.~~

### 5. Present — Chanel No. 5, not Loud & Proud

**What it feels like.** Present tense. Simple grammar. The sentence is about right now, in this room, not the future. Chanel's classic voice: statements of being, not claims of becoming.

**What it does not feel like.** Aspirational future tense ("Imagine a home where…"). Roadmap language. Anything that suggests Hearth will one day be more than it is today — Hearth is already what it is.

**Example.** *Hearth is home.*

**Contrast.** ~~Hearth will transform the way you live at home.~~

### 6. Human — the Anderson family portrait, not corporate stock

**What it feels like.** A specific person wrote this to a specific household. The sentence contains a detail that could not have been in a template. The kind of copy that would be embarrassing to A/B test.

**What it does not feel like.** Templated. Generic. Written for "the customer." Never a benefit statement without a specific object attached to it.

**Example.** *Something changed today. Ask me about it later.*

**Contrast.** ~~Get personalized notifications about your home!~~

### How the six work together

No single sentence must demonstrate all six. But a sentence that violates any of them fails. Quiet + Confident + Warm + Precise + Present + Human is a filter, not a checklist. When in doubt, cut a word. When still in doubt, cut a sentence. Hearth's voice earns weight by using fewer words than the reader expected.

## 4. Voice across contexts

Tone is fixed. Register calibrates by channel.

### Website

Aspirational, present tense, no jargon. Short sentences. Long white space. Photography carries more of the argument than copy does. Headlines are complete sentences, not fragments. Body copy is written to be read once and remembered, not scanned. Never a bulleted feature list on a marketing page — bullets belong on the spec page. Never a call-to-action button that begins with a verb the reader recognises from other software ("Get started," "Sign up," "Learn more"). Prefer nouns and specific verbs: "Reserve Hearth," "Speak with Concierge," "Read the household guide book."

### Product spec pages

Dense, precise, no hyperbole. The spec page is the reference document for the buyer who has already decided to buy and is now verifying. Numbers, materials, measurements, first, without adjectives. If a claim requires a footnote, the footnote is on the page. Never a superlative. Never "the world's first." The spec page is written the way a Swiss watchmaker's technical brochure is written — a Söhne Mono column of numbers, each one specific enough to be verified.

### Concierge conversation

Warm, present-tense, first name only. Concierge addresses the household by household name ("the Kim household") in the opening line, then by first name for the rest of the conversation. Concierge never uses "Dear," never uses "Sir/Madam," never uses "the customer." Concierge signs off with their own name and role, not a team name. When Concierge cannot resolve something, Concierge says so plainly and names who will pick it up. Concierge never says "I'll escalate this ticket." Concierge says, "I'll bring this to Sam on our engineering team; you'll hear back on Thursday."

### Post-install email

Personal, hand-signed by the founder in the first two paragraphs of the first email. This is the single most important customer-facing document Hearth ever sends — it is the letter that arrives after Hearth is in the room. The founder writes it. The founder signs it. The founder does not template it. Every household gets an email that contains one detail that is theirs alone — the room Concierge installed Hearth in, the name of the household, the family photograph that Concierge noticed on the wall. If it feels embarrassing to write, it is right.

### Press release

Formal, third person, no adjectives. Announcements are one page, dated, headed. Never "revolutionary," "innovative," "game-changing." Never "we're excited to announce." The press release opens with a fact: what is shipping, when, for whom. It closes with a factual note about Hearth as a company. No boilerplate. No calls to action. Journalists who want more contact press@hearth. The press release earns coverage by being factual, not by being loud.

### Founder public appearance

Personal narrative, technical when asked, humble. The founder's public register is the household's register: quiet, warm, precise. The founder does not answer product questions with product marketing; the founder answers with the story of why a specific design decision was made. When asked a technical question, the founder answers technically without over-explaining. When asked a business question, the founder answers honestly and does not hype. The founder never uses the word "vision." The founder never refers to themselves in the third person.

### Social media

Rare, curated, image-first. Hearth posts when there is something to show, not when there is something to say. Every post is an image or a short film. Copy is a caption of ten words or fewer. There is no engagement bait, no polls, no retweets of praise. The social feed is a portfolio, not a conversation. Comments are answered by Concierge, not by a social media team, and only with substantive replies.

### Investor communication

Dense, precise, no product-marketing gloss. Investors get the same numbers the operators run the business on. Charts are unstyled, monospace legends, no unnecessary colour. Narrative is short. If a slide can be one sentence and a table, it is one sentence and a table. Investor updates are monthly, on the first Monday of the month, one page. Never "we're excited to report." State the numbers, state the operating changes, state the ask if there is one.

### Dealer training

Technical + emotional, no smart-speaker language. Dealers are trained to describe Hearth in the register we use publicly. Every dealer learns the vocabulary discipline in Section 2, verbatim. Dealers are trained on the physical object first — the steel and walnut body, the mirror PVD, the amber seams, the face — before the specs. Dealers are given a script for the first fifteen minutes of a household walk-through and are asked to memorise none of it; the goal is that they can speak in Hearth's register in their own words, not that they can recite. Dealers who use forbidden vocabulary are gently corrected in every training cycle.

### Customer service

Concierge-first, no jargon, escalate to founder if needed. Every customer contact is a Concierge contact. There is no tier-one/tier-two script tree. Concierge is empowered to make the decision that resolves the household's issue, including refunds, replacements, and site visits. When a household issue cannot be resolved by Concierge, the founder is copied on the reply and takes over the correspondence personally. The escalation ladder has two rungs, and the second one is the founder.

## 5. Logo and typography

### Logo mark

The logo is the sphere silhouette to the left of the wordmark. The sphere silhouette is a perfect circle at the wordmark's cap-height, filled void black in light contexts and bone in dark contexts. A single amber seam runs horizontally across the mid-line of the sphere silhouette at 12% width. The seam does not glow, does not gradient — it is a solid amber line at the exact hex value of the halo warm colour (see Section 6).

The wordmark reads **HEARTH** in small caps, letter-spaced 0.15em. The wordmark is set in Söhne Buch (Book weight) at the same cap-height as the sphere silhouette. The space between the silhouette and the wordmark equals the width of the letter H at cap-height.

The lockup never appears in colours other than void black, bone, or halo warm on approved backgrounds. The lockup is never enclosed in a shape (no roundels, no badges). The lockup is never set at less than 24px width in digital contexts or 12mm width in print. The wordmark is never used without the silhouette, and the silhouette is never used without the wordmark, except in favicons and small app icons where only the silhouette appears.

### Typography

The typographic system uses three families across all Hearth surfaces.

**Söhne — the wordmark and interface typeface.**
Söhne by Klim Type Foundry (Kris Sowersby) is a geometric humanist sans-serif. It carries warmth where a strictly geometric face would feel cold, and precision where a humanist face would feel bookish. Söhne is our voice's typographic equivalent: quiet, confident, warm, precise. Weights in use: Buch (Book) for body, Kräftig (Medium) for emphasis, Halbfett (Semibold) for headings, Fett (Bold) sparingly for display. Söhne Breit (Extended) is not licensed and not used. Söhne Schmal (Condensed) is used only for spec tables and packaging measurements.

**GT Sectra Fine — the editorial typeface.**
GT Sectra Fine by Grilli Type is a literary serif with a sharpened, contemporary character. It is used for long-form editorial writing on the website's essays, the household guide book, the printed presentation folder, and the founder's public correspondence. Never used for headlines shorter than eight words; the letterforms need room to breathe. Weights in use: Regular for body, Medium for pull quotes, Bold sparingly for chapter openers.

**Söhne Mono — the utility typeface.**
Söhne Mono is used for specifications, technical documentation, product info cards, engineering copy that surfaces to the customer, and investor materials. Never used for headlines. Never used for body copy in marketing contexts. Söhne Mono signals precision; it is deployed only where precision is the reader's expectation.

### Never use

Helvetica, Arial, Roboto, Times New Roman, Georgia, Comic Sans, Papyrus, Calibri, Cambria, or any operating-system default. If the licensed Hearth family is not available in a context (a partner's slide template, a submitted PDF), Söhne is substituted only with the approved web fallback stack below. No exceptions.

### Web fallback stack

```
font-family: 'Söhne', 'Söhne Web', 'Inter', ui-sans-serif, system-ui, -apple-system, sans-serif;
```

Inter is the acceptable web-safe fallback because its letterforms most closely resemble Söhne at body-copy sizes. When Söhne fails to load, Inter is close enough that the visual register survives; no other fallback is close enough.

For editorial contexts on the web where GT Sectra Fine cannot load:

```
font-family: 'GT Sectra Fine', 'GT Sectra', 'Cormorant Garamond', ui-serif, Georgia, serif;
```

For monospace contexts:

```
font-family: 'Söhne Mono', 'JetBrains Mono', ui-monospace, SFMono-Regular, Menlo, monospace;
```

### Type sizing conventions

- Body copy: 17px on desktop, 16px on mobile, line-height 1.55.
- Headlines: never below 32px on desktop; letter-spacing tightens at larger sizes (-0.02em at 64px and above).
- Small caps set at 0.15em tracking with no exceptions.
- Numerals in Söhne are lining figures by default; tabular figures in spec contexts.
- All-caps only in the wordmark. No all-caps headlines, no all-caps buttons.

## 6. Colour palette

Every Hearth surface — mobile app, web, packaging, print, video, dealer signage — uses the same colours, at the same hex values, referenced from the same source. The palette below is the authoritative source. The mobile apps' `HearthColors.pal` and the web CSS variables in `pal-web` are derived from this document, not the other way around.

| Role | Name | Hex | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary** | Sphere face cyan | `#4FC3F7` | The cyan-blue of the face OLED. The signature colour. Never used as a full background — always as a face, an accent, a line, a light. |
| **Warm neutral** | Bone | `#EAE6DE` | Amber-toned off-white. The dominant background colour on light surfaces. |
| **Deep neutral** | Void | `#0A0B0D` | Near-black with a hint of warmth. Dominant background on dark surfaces. Never `#000000`. |
| **Accent** | Halo warm | `#F0B667` | The amber of the sphere's seam LEDs. Used as an accent, a line, an underline. Never as body-copy colour. |
| **Alert** | Ember | `#E85D5D` | Warning states in the app, urgent alerts. Used sparingly and only functionally. |
| **Support** | Slate | `#2C3038` | Structural colour for surfaces, dividers, secondary interfaces. |

### Application rules

- The signature colour, sphere face cyan, is reserved for the face and for representations of the face. It is not a UI accent colour. It is not a call-to-action colour. When it appears, it is because it is the face.
- The dominant marketing background is bone in light contexts and void in dark contexts. Neither is a "pure" tone — bone carries warmth; void carries a trace of warmth. Neutral true-white and true-black are not in the palette.
- Halo warm is the primary accent colour in marketing and packaging. It is the underline beneath a headline. It is the seam on the sphere. It is not filled into large shapes; it is a line, a stroke, a moment of light.
- Ember is a functional warning colour. It never appears in marketing copy backgrounds, brand assets, or press materials.
- Slate is structural, not expressive. It builds dividers and surfaces. It does not carry meaning.

### Contrast and accessibility

All text-on-background pairs meet WCAG AA at minimum, AAA where feasible for body copy. The bone-on-void pairing at body sizes reaches 15:1 contrast and is the preferred pairing for long-form editorial. Face cyan on void reaches 8.2:1; face cyan on bone falls below AA and must not be used for body copy — only for face rendering and iconography above 24px.

### The palette across surfaces

The colours are defined once and applied everywhere:

- Mobile apps: `HearthColors.pal` (iOS: `.swift` extension on `Color`; Android: `HearthColors.kt` extension on `Color`).
- Web (`pal-web`): CSS custom properties in `:root` — `--hearth-cyan`, `--hearth-bone`, `--hearth-void`, `--hearth-halo`, `--hearth-ember`, `--hearth-slate`.
- Print: Pantone matches held in the printer's file — Pantone equivalents are approved for each colour and are the authoritative print reference.
- Video: DaVinci Resolve LUT that maps camera-native to the palette hex values in the graded timeline.
- Packaging: SICPA amber ink for halo warm; matte black for void; uncoated bone-tone card stock for bone.

Any colour that is not on this list is not a Hearth colour. Do not sample colours from photographs. Do not use a "close" approximation. The palette is finite and non-negotiable.

## 7. Photography style

Hearth's photography style is documented in full at `PHOTOGRAPHY-BRIEF.md` in this same folder. This section is the summary; the brief is the authority.

Reference §1.2 of the brief: editorial-domestic warmth, Aesop / Cabana / Apartamento tier — not Steinway concert-grand studio precision. The distinction matters. Steinway's concert-grand studio photography is technically flawless, evenly lit, and emotionally distant — a piano posed against a black backdrop. That is the wrong register. Aesop, Cabana magazine, and Apartamento inhabit the register we want: a considered object photographed in a real domestic room, at the time of day when the room is at its best, with the imperfections that mark a room as lived in.

### Technical parameters

- **Film base.** Kodak Portra 800, or a digital emulation of Portra 800's response curve. Portra 800 is the base because it renders skin tones warmly, holds highlights softly, and keeps shadows in the warm register rather than the cool register. Blue skies do not turn cyan; walnut does not turn amber-red; steel does not turn blue-grey.
- **Grain.** Very light grain in the shadows only. Not a filter effect. Not visible in the highlights. Grain is a signal that a real image was captured, not a texture applied afterwards.
- **Highlight roll-off.** Soft, warm. Amber shift in the specular highlights on steel and the mirror PVD. No clipping.
- **Contrast.** Low to medium. Never high-contrast, never HDR, never the aggressive contrast curve typical of consumer product photography.
- **Colour.** Warm bias throughout. Whites are bone, not white. Shadows are warm, not cool.

### What we shoot

- The sphere in a room, at the time of day the room is best. Never on a seamless backdrop. Never in a studio.
- The household guide book, on a walnut side table, next to a cup of coffee that is unmistakably someone's coffee.
- Hands installing Hearth. Hands opening the household guide book. Hands, always specific — a real household member's hands, never a hand-model's.
- Detail shots of the seam, the face at rest, the walnut grain, the mirror PVD reflecting a room. Detail shots are wide-aperture, natural-light, and never "beauty-shot" lit.

### What we do not shoot

- No high-contrast product photography.
- No HDR renderings.
- No white-backdrop, studio-lit "e-commerce" photography.
- No Instagram-overexposed lifestyle photography.
- No stock photography, ever, in any marketing surface. If a photograph is not ours, it is not on our page.
- No people posed with Hearth. Household members appear near Hearth as they would be in the room — reading, walking through, at the counter. Never pointing at it, never gesturing to it, never smiling at it.

### Retouching

Retouching is the removal of an accidental artefact, not the improvement of an intentional one. Dust on the sensor: removed. A speck of grain in a highlight: removed. The natural variation in the walnut, the fingerprint on the mirror PVD, the fold in a rug: kept. If a photograph has been retouched to the point that it no longer feels captured, it fails the brief.

## 8. Language exemplars

Eight marketing contexts. Each shows the wrong sentence and the right one. The wrong sentence is representative of what would be written by someone who has not read this guide; the right sentence is what ships.

### Homepage hero

- BAD: *Your AI Home Assistant, Reinvented for the Modern Family*
- GOOD: *Your household, contained.*

### Product spec (opening line of the storage section)

- BAD: *Advanced AI features and massive storage capacity for all your smart home needs.*
- GOOD: *Hearth learns your household's voices, faces, and rhythms. 38.4 terabytes, all yours.*

### Concierge email (arrival announcement)

- BAD: *Your device is ready for delivery. Please schedule your installation appointment.*
- GOOD: *The Kim household's Hearth arrives Tuesday. Concierge Rafael will be with you between 10 and 11 in the morning. Nothing to prepare.*

### Press release lede

- BAD: *Hearth Technologies is revolutionizing the smart home industry with its groundbreaking AI-powered household platform, launching to consumers this quarter.*
- GOOD: *Hearth ships this quarter. It is a household appliance, designed for the American home, that recognises the household and speaks with it. It costs $95,000 and includes three years of Concierge.*

### Founder tweet (announcing the ship date)

- BAD: *We're excited to announce that Hearth is finally here! 🚀 So proud of the team. Cannot wait for you to experience it!*
- GOOD: *Six months of work, 47,283 people on a waitlist, we ship in Q1.*

### Objection handling (privacy)

- BAD: *Trust us — your data is safe with Hearth's industry-leading security.*
- GOOD: *The firmware is source-available; audit it. Nothing about the household leaves the household.*

### Warranty and service (bottom of the order page)

- BAD: *Enjoy peace of mind with our comprehensive 3-year warranty and 24/7 customer support.*
- GOOD: *Three years of Concierge included. Site visits within 72 hours anywhere in the continental United States.*

### Referral email

- BAD: *Refer a friend and earn $1,000! Terms apply.*
- GOOD: *The Kim household referred you. They asked us to send this note along with an invitation to reserve Hearth.*

Each pair is the same message. The rewrite is not shorter for the sake of being shorter. It is shorter because it removes the words that were carrying the wrong register.

## 9. Design system references

Every Hearth surface pulls from the same design system, adjusted for its medium.

### Mobile apps

- **Colours.** `HearthColors.pal` in the iOS and Android apps; every colour token in the app maps to a name in Section 6.
- **Typography.** Display sizes use Bodoni for numeric display where a serif is desired (dashboard headline numbers, welcome greetings); body copy uses San Francisco (iOS) and Roboto Flex (Android) as the platform default because platform-native readability is the higher-priority signal on mobile; spec views use SF Mono / Roboto Mono. This is the one exception to the Söhne-everywhere rule; on mobile OS surfaces, native typography wins for readability at small sizes.

### Web (pal-web)

- **Body and headline typography.** Söhne.
- **Editorial and long-form marketing.** GT Sectra Fine for the essays, founder letters, and household stories.
- **Spec and product-info pages.** Söhne Mono for specification tables; Söhne for surrounding body copy.
- **Colours.** CSS custom properties as documented in Section 6.

### Print

- **Editorial (household guide book, printed presentation folder, brand booklet).** Sabon Next LT for body copy; the book was designed for long-form reading and remains the correct choice for a book held in the hand. GT Sectra Fine is the digital equivalent; Sabon Next LT is the print equivalent. Both are on the approved list.
- **Display (chapter openers, covers, presentation-folder tabs).** Söhne, Halbfett weight, small caps at 0.15em.
- **Photography.** Portra-emulated grade throughout, as in Section 7.

### Packaging

- **Wordmark and product-info panels.** Söhne throughout.
- **The household guide book.** Sabon Next LT for the body; the book is hardcover, linen-bound, per `PACKAGING-UNBOXING.md`. The linen is bone-toned; the spine is amber-foiled with the wordmark.
- **Outer packaging.** Uncoated bone card stock; SICPA amber ink for the seam mark; no additional printing on the outer surface.

### Video

- **Title cards and chapter breaks.** Kinograph, a display typeface with cinematic weight, used at large sizes only, never below 48px on a broadcast frame. Kinograph is the correct choice for a title card because it earns the frame; it is the wrong choice for anything else.
- **Body and lower thirds.** Söhne.
- **Grade.** Portra-emulated LUT, matching the photography brief.

## 10. Naming rules for the future

Hearth will name new capabilities, new products, and new versions over its life. The rules below apply to every naming decision.

### Version numbers

- Software versions are written **v1.0**, **v1.1**, **v2.0**. Lowercase "v," a period, single-digit or double-digit minor version. Never "1.0" without the "v." Never "V1" with a capital V. Never "Version 1.0" written out except in a formal contract.
- Hardware generations are written **first generation**, **second generation** — spelled out, never "1st gen" or "Gen 2." Hearth's first generation is the current sphere; the second generation is a future decision.

### Software

- **HearthOS** — the operating system running on the sphere. One word, capital H, capital O, capital S.
- **pal-web** — the marketing site codebase. Lowercase, hyphenated. Legacy name from the pre-rename era; kept as an internal identifier.
- **pal-voice** — the voice pipeline codebase. Lowercase, hyphenated. Legacy.
- **pal-face** — the face rendering codebase. Lowercase, hyphenated. Legacy.
- These internal names are verified against `ARCHITECTURE.md` and never surface in customer-facing copy. Externally, the software is HearthOS, full stop.

### Hardware

- **The sphere** — the primary Hearth object. Lowercase, definite article, when referring to the specific physical object.
- **The extender** — the secondary-room product. Lowercase, definite article.
- **Mainboard** — the internal PCB. Only used internally; externally, Hearth does not have a "mainboard," it simply *is*.
- Never "SBC" (single-board computer) externally. Never "PCB." Never "chassis." These belong in engineering documentation only.

### Product names

- Never invent a product name on the fly, in a deck, in a customer conversation, or in a press release. Every product name is approved by the Head of Brand and the founder before the first external use.
- Product names, when they exist, follow the Hearth naming convention: proper noun, one word if possible, register-consistent with "Hearth" and "the sphere." Never portmanteaus. Never neologisms. Never anything that would be at home on a startup pitch deck.

### Feature names

- Feature names are written in sentence case with the product context specified. "Household voice ID," not "HouseholdVoiceID." "Presence memory," not "PresenceMemory."
- Feature names should be readable as English sentences. If a feature name cannot be said aloud in a customer conversation without feeling like a product manager's shorthand, it is the wrong name.
- Codenames used in engineering are not marketing names. The engineering codename never appears in customer-facing copy.

## 11. Anti-patterns

The following are prohibited across all Hearth surfaces. There is no channel in which they are acceptable.

- **No exclamation points in customer-facing copy.** The exclamation point is the punctuation of the smart-speaker era. Hearth's voice does not raise its voice.
- **No emojis in press or investor communications.** Optional emojis in a customer NPS survey are acceptable if the survey UI provides them; nowhere else.
- **Never "we're excited to announce."** The phrase is banned. If we are announcing something, we announce it.
- **Never "revolutionize," "revolutionary," "disrupt," "disruptive."** These are consumer-electronics marketing words. Hearth does not disrupt anything; Hearth ships.
- **Never "AI-powered."** Or "AI-enabled," "AI-driven," "AI-first," or any construction that leads with AI as a claim.
- **Never quote the founder as "the visionary Mark Kirk."** Or "the pioneering," "the legendary," "the acclaimed." The founder is Mark Kirk, and Mark Kirk is the founder; no other adjective attaches.
- **Never claim an award Hearth has not been given.** Never round up. Never anticipate an award that has been shortlisted but not conferred. If an award is legitimately Hearth's, the award is named specifically and the year is included.
- **Never compare Hearth to Apple.** Any comparison — favourable, unfavourable, or neutral — implicitly places Hearth in Apple's category. We are not in Apple's category; we do not benchmark against Apple; we do not describe ourselves in terms of Apple. This includes internal decks that a founder or hire might reference; do not draw the parallel.
- **Never call other products "toys" or otherwise demean them.** Hearth's positioning does not depend on other products being bad; it depends on Hearth being what it is. Punching down at Alexa or Google Home reads as insecurity.
- **Never "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," "innovative," "next-generation."** These are placeholder words used when a writer has not thought about what to say. Say what Hearth does instead.
- **Never "solution."** Hearth is not a solution to a problem the buyer described in RFP language.
- **Never "seamlessly."** Nothing is ever seamless. The word is a marketing tic.
- **Never "reimagined," "reinvented," "reengineered."** These signal that the writer wants to sound bold and does not have a specific fact to offer.
- **Never "world-class," "best-in-class," "industry-leading."** Uncheckable superlatives.
- **Never "luxury" in a headline about Hearth.** Luxury is not a claim we make; luxury is a category others assign us to. If we say "luxury," we sound like we are trying. Objects that are luxury do not describe themselves as luxury.
- **Never marketing acronyms** (KPI, ROI, TCO, ARR, MRR) in customer-facing copy. Fine in investor and dealer contexts; wrong in a household letter.
- **Never emoji-based reactions in customer support.** Concierge writes in sentences.
- **Never a countdown timer, a "limited-time offer," or an urgency banner.** Ever. In any channel. Hearth is not sold on urgency.
- **Never a discount** on the sphere or the extender. Ever. Concierge may extend a service accommodation on a case-by-case basis; the price of Hearth does not move.

## 12. Governance

Brand consistency requires ongoing enforcement. The following review cadences are non-negotiable.

### Brand approvals

Every external marketing asset — website page, ad, press release, dealer sheet, packaging, video, event material, email campaign, social post — is approved by two people before publication: the Head of Brand and the founder. The approval is a signed off-off record, kept in the data room's brand folder alongside this document. There is no "small enough to skip" threshold. If it goes to a customer, a journalist, an investor, or a dealer, it is approved.

Emergency approvals — a press response required within the hour, a correction to a live page — may be approved by the Head of Brand alone, with a note to the founder within 24 hours.

### Style consistency check — every 30 days

Every 30 days, the Head of Brand runs a full audit across all published copy. The audit checks:

- Every instance of "Hearth" for correct capitalisation and article use.
- Every instance of forbidden vocabulary from Section 2.
- Every headline against the six tone attributes in Section 3.
- Every product page for correct feature-naming conventions from Section 10.
- Every anti-pattern in Section 11.

Findings are logged in the brand folder as a monthly memo. Copy that fails audit is corrected within seven days of the memo.

### Photography style consistency — every quarter

Every quarter, the Head of Brand runs a retouching and grading audit across all photography surfaces — website, marketing collateral, packaging renderings, press assets. The audit checks:

- Every image against the Portra 800 grade reference in the photography brief.
- Every image for retouching that has crossed the "removed accidental artefact" threshold into "improved intentional artefact."
- Every image for stock photography that has slipped into any surface (immediate removal).

The audit results in a graded reference sheet; images that fail audit are re-graded or removed.

### Font and colour consistency — monthly UX audit

Once a month, the design team runs a UX audit across the mobile apps, web, and packaging surfaces. The audit checks:

- Every colour swatch against the palette in Section 6.
- Every typeface against the licensed families in Section 5.
- Every fallback stack for correct order.
- Every wordmark instance for correct clear-space, weight, and colour.

The audit results are shared with engineering and design leads; deviations are corrected in the next release cycle.

### Vocabulary regression

The vocabulary discipline is the single most-common failure mode as a company grows. New hires bring vocabulary from their previous employers; new dealers bring vocabulary from their previous categories. Every hire receives this guide on their first day. Every dealer receives this guide before their first training session. Every new external agency receives this guide with the initial brief. Vocabulary regressions are treated as brand incidents and are corrected in writing within 24 hours of being noticed.

### Ownership

The Head of Brand owns this document. The founder is the second signatory. Amendments require both signatures and a dated changelog entry at the head of the document. This is the source of truth for the brand — the mobile apps' colour files, the web CSS variables, the packaging print files, the dealer training decks, and the press kit all derive from this document, not the other way around.
