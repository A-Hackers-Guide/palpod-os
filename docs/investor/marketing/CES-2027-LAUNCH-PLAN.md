## 1. Trade Show Strategy — Why CES 2027 Matters for Hearth Specifically

CES 2027 is not a general show for Hearth. It is a targeted, four-day operation to compress twelve months of luxury-AV go-to-market work into a single physical location where our exact buyers, channel partners, and press already are.

**Why CES is the correct venue.** The luxury AV integrator (LAI) channel — Crestron, Control4, Savant, Josh.ai, Elan, Vantage, URC, Loxone — does not attend CEDIA in September in numbers that matter for a Series A launch; CEDIA is 90% installer-day, not principal-day. CES is where the *principals* — the founders, VPs of dealer development, and the heads of luxury programs — walk the floor between January 6 and 9. It is also the only week of the year when Nilay Patel, Sean Hollister, David Pierce, Joanna Stern, Brian X. Chen, Ryan Waniata, and Julian Chokkattu are all in the same square mile and hungry for a story that isn't another Samsung Frame refresh or LG rollable OLED. And it is the moment when the annual "this is the year of home AI" narrative gets set — usually by two or three companies who show up with hardware that photographs well.

Hearth is one of those companies for 2027. The Shark Tank episode aired Q4 2026 and generated 47,000 concierge waitlist entries (per POST-AIR-PR-PLAYBOOK metrics) and closed the Series A at $60M post in January 2027. CES is where we convert Shark Tank narrative into channel structure and Series B optionality.

**Why CES is not the correct venue for everything.** CES is *not* where retail consumers buy. It is *not* where mass-market brands (Google, Amazon, Anker) actually announce products — they announce elsewhere and use CES as reinforcement. And it is *not* where luxury clients themselves show up; those clients hear about Hearth from their integrator, their interior designer, or Robb Report. So the booth is designed as a **B2B enablement environment**, not a consumer demo pit. This distinction drives every design and staffing decision below.

**Target outcomes, ranked (downgraded from v1 to defensible levels — see §10).**

1. **200+ scheduled 1:1 meetings** with LAI integrators, of which 10 convert to signed dealer program agreements and an additional 25 to non-binding letters of interest within 90 days.
2. **10 tech press pieces** across the target reporter list, of which at least 2 are feature-length (not roundup mentions).
3. **3–5 competitive-intelligence conversations** with principals from Sonos, Josh.ai, Crestron, and Devialet — booth walks and off-record dinners.
4. **20+ Series B investor conversations**, with a realistic mix of consumer-hardware VCs, corporate strategics, and family offices with an actual consumer-hardware thesis (see §5 for the corrected list).
5. **40+ hand-raised prospects** for the private client program (concierge waitlist entries directly from booth intake).
6. **Editorial "moment of CES 2027" mention (aspirational, not planned).** If Verge, Wired, or Fast Company post-CES roundup pieces name Hearth as one of the two or three "moments" of the week, that outcome is earned, not scheduled. It is called out here because if it lands, it recovers the entire booth spend.

Any single one of the first four outcomes recovers a meaningful multiple of the descoped $450k booth spend. Two of them together fund the entire Q1 marketing budget. Three of them together mean Hearth is the second-most-photographed product at CES.

## 2. Booth Location + Design

**Location.** 30′×40′ island booth in the Palazzo Ballroom at the Venetian Expo — CES Village Level, high-end AV cluster. The Palazzo Ballroom is where Sonos, McIntosh Group, MartinLogan, and Focal-Naim have historically shown; it is *adjacent* to but not part of the Central Hall madness at the LVCC. This placement communicates category before we say a word. The Palazzo Ballroom is the industry-designated luxury-AV cluster; the buyer intent is qualitatively higher than LVCC main hall foot traffic per our Freeman rep confirmation (Sept 2026 call). We are not publishing specific qualified-traffic percentages — the "60-70% vs. 15-20%" framing from v1 was directional, not sourced, and was flagged by the verifier. The category signal from the room itself is the argument; we do not need a manufactured statistic.

The block is close enough to Josh.ai's suite (Venetian Level 3, historically) for principals to walk between us, which is exactly what we want. Booth space (30×40 island, all four sides open) is $80–95k for the 4 days per current CTA/Freeman rate card, up from the $65k line in v1 — premium island footprints in the Palazzo are priced above the general island average and go early. Confirmation on availability of the specific block is needed from Freeman by mid-September 2026.

**Design intent.** The booth is a recreation of a luxury great-room in a $12M Aspen or Big Sur home. Not a stage. Not a demo pit. A place a Crestron principal will linger in for 20 minutes because it feels like an actual home, and a place Nilay Patel will photograph because it doesn't look like the rest of CES.

**Materials — real, not printed.**
- Floor: reclaimed white-oak plank (from Stuga or Chestnut Specialists), 1,200 sq ft, delivered on pallet. Installed at $18–30/sqft = $22–36k for the floor alone. Not stick-on vinyl.
- One accent wall: 8′ tall, honed Calacatta Viola limestone, backlit from behind by a 2700K LED strip. Sourced from ABC Stone in Long Island City, cut to booth spec. Fabricated wall installed with backlit LED: $18–40k.
- Structural: hot-rolled blackened steel columns (four corners of the central plinth), fabricated by a Los Angeles metalshop (Neal Feay or comparable). Powder-coated matte black, no logo. $15–40k for the fabrication package.
- Lighting: no overhead conference-hall fluorescents. We hang our own — 14 track-mounted 2700K Erco spots at 8′ height, all dimmable to 30% at press-photo hour, plus one architectural pendant over the demo chairs. Erco spots run $500–900 each in fixtures alone ($7–13k in hardware before rigging).
- Seating: 6 CH25 wishbone chairs (Carl Hansen & Søn, real oak), 1 Barcelona chair (Knoll) in the VIP corner, 1 low walnut coffee table (Bassam Fellows), 1 sideboard for the whiskey bar.
- No pipe-and-drape. Anywhere.

**Signage rule: nothing above 4 feet.** Every conference booth in Vegas is a screaming 12-foot backlit logo. Ours has no backlit logo, no floor graphics, no sky-facing signage. The only Hearth marks are: (a) a single 2″-wide brass plaque at the base of the central plinth reading "HEARTH — no. 47 of 500," and (b) a low, freestanding brushed-brass sign near the entry that reads "HEARTH, at home." That's it. The scarcity of signage is the signage.

**Central plinth.** 42″ tall, walnut top, blackened steel base, 24″ diameter. A single working Hearth sphere sits (with a live Halbach levitation array underneath, 6mm float) at eye height for the seated demo audience. No security dome. It floats in the open. This is deliberate — the sphere's presence in the physical world is the entire product.

**Four seating zones.**

- **Zone A — Main demo.** 2 wishbone chairs facing the sphere at 8′. Behind the chairs, a 65″ Sony A95L OLED mounted at seated eye-level (36″ off floor, not conference-height 60″), acting as the "extender" TV for the demo. This is where the 3-minute demo runs, 4× per hour.
- **Zone B — Distributor discussion.** A small round walnut meeting table with 4 chairs. Set apart from the demo by the limestone wall. Water glasses, no branded pads. This is where every distributor conversation gets sat down and closed to a next step.
- **Zone C — Press interview.** Single wishbone chair opposite a low table, one pendant light, matte black backdrop wall behind (this is our press portrait wall — every reporter and every founder photo comes out looking like an Alec Soth portrait, not a CES headshot). 1080p and 4K cameras on quiet tripods 6′ away, always rolling with visible red light.
- **Zone D — VIP corner.** Screened off with a wool curtain (charcoal, Kvadrat Divina). One Barcelona chair, one side table. Small sideboard bar with five bottles — no visible display marketing: **Suntory Yamazaki 18, Hibiki 21, Michter's 10 Bourbon, Redbreast 27, and Rémy Louis XIII (single pour, for the whales)**. Two Riedel glasses. A refurbished Thorens TD-160 turntable playing side A of Bill Evans *Sunday at the Village Vanguard* on a continuous rotation. This corner is reserved for founder + one guest, invitation only, no walk-ins. It exists for one reason: to convert Series B principals in a 30-minute conversation that ends "we're leading the Series B."

## 3. Booth Staffing (5-person permanent, corrected from v1)

The v1 4-person staffing model was flagged as a single-point-of-failure risk on the Head of Product role during press pulls, and as an over-scoped role for the Concierge Experience Lead. The corrected staffing adds a fifth permanent member and cross-trains two additional backups for the hero demo.

**Permanent 5-person team, all four days.**

1. **Mark Kirk, Founder + CEO.** On the floor 10am–12pm and 2pm–4pm daily. **Enforced 2-hour midday recovery block, 12:00–14:00, every day.** No exceptions. Mark is out of the booth, off his phone, and either napping in a hotel room or eating quietly. This is the single biggest change from v1 — the verifier flagged 14–16 hour Mark-days as a demo-quality and closing-quality risk by Day 3 afternoon, and they are right. Keynote-hour presence: Tuesday morning (during the Samsung and LG keynotes, when press is off-schedule and floor is empty, Mark is available for the deepest press). Runs the VIP corner during investor blocks. Present at every private event. Dress: charcoal wool suit (no tie), grey merino t-shirt, brown suede lace-up.

2. **Head of Product** (currently open req — needs to be filled by November 2026). Runs the 3-minute demo. Handles all "how does it work" questions from press and integrators. Backup on the Halbach explanation and on the compute stack tour. **Rotated off demo duty during press deep-dives — Sales Engineer covers.**

3. **Head of Sales / Distribution.** Owns the distributor pipeline. Every meeting in Zone B is either run by or co-run by this person. Owns the DocuSign for signed dealer agreements on-site. Target: 10 signed dealer program agreements + 25 non-binding LOIs across the 4-day show + 30-day follow-up window (downgraded from the v1 "30 MOUs in 30 days" fiction — LAI legal cycles are 3–6 months and Cooley LLP's own review says 30-day binding closes will not survive counter-party legal review).

4. **Concierge Experience Lead** (from the private client program). Runs the "household" demo — the memory montage, the family recognition, the multi-user handoff. This is the human face of the concierge program that a $95k buyer signs up for. **Cross-trained as demo backup for Head of Product.** Also handles VIP corner walk-ups; mug gifting is offloaded to the press liaison during peak hours to prevent role overload.

5. **Sales Engineer (new permanent role).** Covers the hero demo when Head of Product is pulled for a press deep-dive. Owns the technical Q&A during distributor meetings — the "how does the Halbach array actually work" and "what's the local model quantization" questions that would otherwise stall a Head of Sales conversation. Backup on the compute stack tour. This role is the single most important addition versus v1 — it removes the demo single-point-of-failure and lets the Head of Product move freely between press and floor.

**Rotating 2-person, 2 days each.**

- **Days 1–2: Firmware engineer.** For the technical press deep-dives (Ars Technica's Ron Amadeo, MKBHD's producer, Verge's Alex Cranz on the compute stack story). Handles anything from local model quantization to the security enclave design. **Cross-trained as demo backup #2.**
- **Days 3–4: Industrial designer.** For the Wallpaper*, Cool Hunting, Dezeen, and Robb Report angles — the material choices, the levitation engineering, the "no logo" philosophy. **Cross-trained as demo backup #3.**

**Support staff.**

- **1 press liaison.** External PR support, embedded with the booth. Owns the reporter calendar, walks reporters in, sits in on interviews, tracks quotes. Also owns mug gifting during peak floor hours (offloaded from Concierge). Likely from Bateman Group, VSC, or an independent (Brooke Hammerling / Brew PR).
- **1 photographer.** Adam Amengual or Andy Ryan tier (architectural / product / editorial). Shoots the booth in Vegas dawn light on Day 0 (setup day, Jan 5), captures every founder-press interaction, delivers 200 images in a shared Dropbox by Day 5 for immediate press use.
- **1 videographer.** Documentary-style, not corporate-highlight-reel — but at a real 4-day shoot + edit budget of $30k (not the v1 $8k Casey-Neistat-tier fantasy). Captures the demos, the reactions, the two or three unexpected moments that become the post-CES sizzle reel. Delivers a 90-second Twitter/LinkedIn edit by Jan 12 and a 3-minute long-form cut by Jan 20.

**Dress code — enforced.** Quiet luxury only. Heather grey merino wool. Navy cashmere. Brown or oxblood leather. No visible logos, on anything. No lanyards inside the booth. Everyone gets a $600 wardrobe stipend, pre-approved.

**Total booth-week staff on site: 10 people** (5 permanent + 2 rotating + 3 support). Unchanged headcount total from v1, but re-distributed to remove the single-point-of-failure on demo delivery.

## 4. Key Demos + Touchpoints

Four moments repeat throughout the day. Each one is a *scene*, staged, rehearsed, and timed. Booth rehearsal happens on Jan 5, all day.

**The 3-Minute Hero Demo.** Runs 4× per hour, on the hour, on the :15, :30, and :45. The concierge sits in Zone A across from a member of the press or an integrator. The sphere wakes to a household question — "Where did we spend Thanksgiving 2024?" The sphere replies in voice, and the extender TV in the same booth surfaces a synchronized photo montage. Then the concierge pulls out an iPad and shows the family app updating in real time with the shared moment. Total runtime: 2:50, plus 30 seconds of Q&A. This is the Shark Tank hero moment, recreated exactly, on the same script we drilled for taping. Every 3-minute demo is a chance to sign one integrator. **Delivery rotation:** Head of Product is primary; Sales Engineer, Concierge, firmware engineer, and industrial designer are trained backups in priority order. Any two of the five can run the demo at any hour of any day.

**The 60-Second Levitation Moment.** On a triggered cadence (every 20 minutes, sync'd to draw crowds), the sphere's Halbach array is dropped to zero and the sphere is caught by hand. The industrial designer (or firmware engineer) picks it up, walks it around the booth, and returns it. The re-levitation is the visual moment — the sphere hovers back into position on its own. This is the "no compute inside, just the OLED face" narrative moment. Press *loves* this shot. It is the single most photographed 60 seconds of the booth week.

**The 15-Second Competitive Card.** A single 24″×18″ printed card (letterpress, cotton stock, no logo) sits on the Zone B table. It's a feature matrix — Hearth vs. Josh.ai / Crestron / Home Assistant / Sonos — across nine dimensions (offline capability, per-household training, multi-user memory, physical security, whole-home AI, custom hardware, subscription-free, extender streaming, VIP concierge). Every distributor walks out having seen it. No voice-over, no pitch — the card sells itself in 15 seconds. Sourced from COMPETITIVE-TEARDOWN.md.

**The Tap-and-Consent Demo.** Physical proof of the session-scoped consent model for remote desktop control. A support engineer (playing the role of a remote helper) attempts to connect. The sphere illuminates and asks for consent. The concierge taps the top of the sphere. The connection opens. When the concierge lifts a finger, it closes. This makes the "we are not a spy in your living room" claim *visual*, which is what family offices and integrators need to walk away with. This is our answer to the Josh.ai "we send everything to the cloud" objection, and to the Sonos "your mic is always on" objection. Runs on request in Zone B.

**The Compute Stack Tour.** Zone A includes a second sphere with a transparent side panel — you can see the 6 custom PCBs, the two Ryzen AI 9 HX 370 modules, the ten Jetson Orin NX modules, the passive-cooled thermal spine. A FLIR C5 thermal camera streams a live feed of the operating temperature to a small 5″ display beside it (55–62°C under load). This is the "10 Jetsons + 10 Ryzens in one box" moment. It is the answer to the "why $95k" question in a single glance. Reporters walk away with the exact detail they need to write the compute story.

## 10. Success Metrics + Downside Plan

**Success — what we are targeting (all downgraded from v1 to defensible levels).**

- **200 scheduled meetings** across the 4 days (integrators + press + investors + strategics + walk-ups).
- **10 signed dealer program agreements + 25 non-binding LOIs** within 30 days of show (v1 said "30 signed MOUs" — that was a fiction against 3–6-month LAI legal cycles).
- **10 tech press pieces** across the target reporter list, of which **at least 2 must be feature-length** (v1 said 15 pieces with 3 features — stretch; corrected to 8–10 roundup + 2 features, which is what Verge/Wired/NYT feature cycles actually deliver in a 4–8-week post-CES window with editorial buy-in from months earlier).
- **20 Series B investor conversations**, of which 5 convert to real diligence process by end of Q1.
- **40 private-client-program hand-raises** at the booth, feeding the concierge waitlist.
- **$250k Q1 CES-attributable revenue** + **$500k H1 CES-attributable revenue** (rescaled from v1's $500k Q1, which assumed a dealer onboarding + first-ship cycle that does not exist for a new $95k SKU inside 90 days).

**Aspirational, not planned.**

- **The Verge post-CES roundup names Hearth as a "moment" of CES 2027.** This is entirely dependent on Verge editorial. It cannot be planned. It is called out here as an aspiration — if it lands, it is worth roughly the full Scenario A booth spend in narrative equity. It is not a metric we commit to.

**Downside plan — if Scenario A ($900k) does not clear the board.**

**Execute Scenario B, the descoped $450k plan.** Full spec in §8. The trade-offs:

*What to cut:*
- 20×20 modular booth instead of 30×40 custom fabrication. Reusable components let us amortize into CEDIA 2027 and CES 2028.
- Prima Suite investor breakfast → replaced with a post-CES Zoom the week after, hosted by Mark for the corrected VC list. Loses the "in-room family-office energy" but preserves the ~15 conversations that actually matter.
- Custom Amengual-tier photography → use existing Shark Tank library and post-launch product photos. Loses the "editorial polish" but is invisible in text-only press coverage.
- Documentary videography from $30k full crew down to $12k solo shooter with Sony FX3. Loses the polish; keeps the 90-second social edit.
- Staff on site from 10 to 6 (Concierge and one rotating specialist stay at HQ; firmware engineer + industrial designer still rotate 2-days-each).
- Letterpress prints cut; mugs go from 500 to 300 in circulation.
- Wardrobe stipend held.

*What to preserve:*
- Palazzo Ballroom placement. This is category signal; downgrading to a smaller Central Hall booth undoes the entire narrative.
- Signed dealer program agreements pipeline. Head of Sales, Sales Engineer, and the Zone B conversation flow all survive at 20×20 footprint.
- Press dinner at Mott 32. This is the single highest-ROI event of the week. Do not cut.
- Distributor cocktail — moved to a smaller Palazzo suite at $18k instead of $30–38k. Reduces headcount from 40 to 25 seats but preserves the format.
- The hero demo, the levitation moment, the compute stack tour. All four moments run identically on a 20×20 footprint.
- Tap-and-consent demo. Same footprint requirement.
- Wardrobe standard (quiet luxury). Free.

*What the pitch becomes:*
- Narrative shifts from "we recreated a $12M great room" to "we built the most editorial, curated, deliberately-not-a-CES-booth booth at CES." This is a real story and plays especially well with Wallpaper*, Dezeen, Cool Hunting, and Robb Report. It plays less well with Bloomberg (Gurman wants scale) and Fast Company (Wilson wants ambition). Net: the descoped pitch survives with 7–9 press pieces instead of 10, and with 1–2 features instead of 2.
- Series B validation weakens by ~15–20% versus Scenario A, because the family-office breakfast is off-site. Mitigated by the follow-up Zoom + a small in-person Sun Valley visit in February.

**Alternate downside plan — if CES itself underperforms** (regardless of scenario chosen).

Definition of underperformance: fewer than 100 scheduled meetings across 4 days, fewer than 4 press pieces, fewer than 5 signed dealer agreements. In that scenario:

- Immediately reallocate the remaining Q1 marketing budget away from further trade shows and into (a) targeted digital + programmatic on WSJ, Robb Report, HTSI, Departures; (b) a Sotheby's partnership (a curated single-lot auction of a limited-edition sphere as the marketing event); (c) direct private-client-program outreach via the family-office network cultivated at Sun Valley + Vail winter events.
- Cancel the planned CEDIA 2027 booth investment and redirect to a smaller CEDIA hospitality suite ($40k vs $180k full booth).
- Book the founder onto 6 podcast recordings over the following 60 days (Decoder, Waveform, Hard Fork, Sharp Tech, Stratechery, The Talk Show), which are the highest-ROI substitute for CES press coverage.

**Key operational risk: the empty-booth risk.**

The single largest failure mode is a 30×40 booth with three hours of dead air because the Josh.ai suite 40 yards away is drawing the same crowd. Mitigation:

- **Four timed sphere reveals per hour, on the :00, :15, :30, and :45.** Halbach re-levitation, done in silence, with all track lighting dimmed to 30%, always draws a crowd from adjacent booths.
- **The Named Greeting hook.** Weird, filmable, shareable.
- **The whiskey bar in the VIP corner.** Pre-scheduled 15-minute VIP visits keep the booth occupied at the deepest lulls.
- **Founder floor time.** Mark walks the floor twice a day (Sonos, Devialet, McIntosh, Josh.ai, Master & Dynamic, Focal) during his active hours (not during the 12:00–14:00 recovery block). Every time he's seen at another booth, an integrator walks back to ours.

**Key narrative risk: press writes the "$95k for a smart speaker" story.**

Mitigation is upstream and offense-driven. The competitive teardown one-pager and the "10 Jetsons + 10 Ryzens" moment need to reach every reporter before their first draft. Head of Product and Sales Engineer both own the technical framing in press interviews. On-message answer: *"This is not a smart speaker. This is a household compute environment that happens to have voice on the front. Compare it to a Crestron install, not a HomePod."* Rehearse this in Jan 5 booth walkthrough.

**Key channel risk: a major LAI (Josh.ai or Crestron) attempts to buy or block.**

Mitigation is scenario-planned before we arrive. If a Josh.ai or Crestron principal asks for an exclusive discussion, Mark takes the meeting in the VIP corner, listens for 30 minutes, and commits to nothing on site. Any acquisition or exclusive-distribution overtures route to Cooley LLP within 24 hours. The board has pre-approved: no acquisition conversations before the Series B close.
